# The Nuradyne Papers

Run 2 | Writer: sonnet | Emphasis: Kira Voight as deuteragonist

## Logline

Sent to investigate a whisper of a signal, Ryker finds a city built on a slow-motion atrocity — and a
Chief of Security who has spent three years building the case against her own employer from the
inside, one quiet favor at a time, waiting for someone with nothing to lose to finish what she started.

## How this version reads

Every run-1 writer put the twist in front of the player. This one puts it in front of Ryker *and*
Kira Voight at the same time, from opposite sides of the same locked door, and asks what each of them
does when they finally see the whole shape of it. Voight is not a mirror-universe double, not a
mustache-twirling company loyalist, and not a late-breaking convert who exists to hand Ryker an
ending. She has been running her own quiet counter-investigation inside Nuradyne for three years,
for reasons that have nothing to do with the FMA and everything to do with a man she watched die.
She is smarter than Ryker in this city, she got there first, and she has more to lose. The player's
relationship with her — interrogated by her, recruited by her, recruiting her, or burying her — is
the spine the plot hangs on.

## What I take as fixed vs. what I invented

**Fixed (canon, unchanged):** Ryker as blank-slate ex-military operator; the FMA and its outpost; the
transversal-anomaly hook; Quinmore/U-7134 and the dome-and-poison-air setup; the Corporate Union and
its per-corporation jurisdiction; Nuradyne's reactors as multiversal siphons; Atlas as the distrustful
neighbor; the slum reactor explosion, the operative, the cordon-and-checkpoint cover story, the
relocation warehouse; the stranded FMA liaison who sold Nuradyne the tech and was later cut loose;
Director Harmon suppressing the "abnormal readings" reports; the missing slum kid; the Prologue and
Quinmore-arrival beats verbatim, including the mother at the checkpoint and the datacube naming
Harmon; the Aire Gardens multiport-to-subway level from `Ideas.md`; the player-verb constraints.

**Invented for this story:** Kira Voight's full backstory and arc (her predecessor Aldric Season, her
private arrangement with the liaison, her three-year shadow file); the liaison's name (Feodor Kessler)
and his specific relationship to Voight; the operative's name and goal (Threnody Vask, pursuing a
"correction" rather than simple revenge or escape); the FMA's institutional hidden agenda (extraction,
not just observation); a CU board fixer named Priya Okonkwo-Lindqvist who represents the board's
interest in the story staying containable; a slum syndicate broker, Dutch Marsh, who deals with
Nuradyne's black budget; the Aire Gardens subway relay Vask is rigging; four endings.

## Factions & power structure

- **Nuradyne.** Runs Quinmore's power. Publicly a utility; privately the custodian of a secret that
  would end the Corporate Union's legitimacy if it got out — its reactors don't generate power, they
  steal it, from universes that have no way to object. The inner circle is small: Harmon, Voight, two
  reactor engineers who "follow protocol" without grasping the full picture, and the CEO, who signs
  what Harmon puts in front of her and asks nothing. Nuradyne's leverage over the CU board is the
  leverage of a company that controls the thing that keeps everyone else's air breathable.
- **Atlas Subsystems.** Dome shielding. Publicly blames Nuradyne for the security lapse that let the
  slum dome fail. Privately suspects a Nuradyne power play and is quietly gathering its own leverage —
  Atlas would like nothing more than a legitimate reason to seize partial control of the power grid
  "for public safety." Atlas doesn't know what Nuradyne is hiding. It assumes it's something venal,
  not something civilization-ending, and would use it exactly the same way either way.
- **The CU board.** A committee of corporate reps with no appetite for a scandal that threatens the
  entire dome system's legitimacy. Represented on the ground by fixers like Okonkwo-Lindqvist, whose
  job is making sure whatever happened in the slums gets a tidy, boring explanation before it becomes
  a governance crisis. The board doesn't know about the multiverse. It would rather not know about
  anything.
- **The slums and the syndicates.** People living in the gap between corporate jurisdictions, where
  Nuradyne's authority is real but its interest is thin. Dutch Marsh runs a smuggling network that
  moves people, drugs, and information across the checkpoint lines — and, it turns out, has been
  quietly paid by Nuradyne's security budget for years to keep an eye on one particular asset: Kessler,
  the liaison, who lives under Marsh's protection without knowing who's really paying for it.
- **The FMA.** Ryker's employer. Publicly a peacekeeping authority. Its stated mission for this
  operation is assessment, not intervention — gather intelligence, determine scope, report back. Its
  actual mission, revealed at the midpoint, is broader: if Nuradyne's siphon technology works, the
  FMA's own multiversal infrastructure — which spans and serves member-Earths across hundreds of
  universes and consumes enormous energy doing it — wants it too, under controlled conditions the FMA
  would set for itself and no one else.
- **The drained universes.** Plural, though the story only ever meets one of them in the flesh:
  Threnody Vask's world, which traced the siphon home and sent her through the aperture. Not a
  monolith, not "the enemy" — a dying civilization that ran out of peaceful options roughly a decade
  before Vask's mission and has been arguing internally ever since about whether retaliation, rescue,
  or surrender is the only move left that matters.

## Key characters

**Liam Ryker.** Unchanged in affect and method. This story doesn't try to crack him open; it puts
pressure on his stated ethic — ends justify the means, just business — by making the "means" a person
he has to look at across a table more than once.

**Kira Voight — Nuradyne Chief of Security.** Mid-forties, promoted into the job eight years ago by
her mentor and predecessor, Aldric Season, who ran reactor security through the *previous* round of
"abnormal readings" — the incident Harmon mentions having handled before this one. Season started
asking questions after that incident. Six months later he died in what Nuradyne's own report called a
containment-suit failure during a routine facility walk-through. Voight signed off on the report
because she was twenty-eight and new and had no evidence, only a feeling, and a company that closes
ranks around its own equipment failures with unusual speed. She has spent every year since building
the case she couldn't build at twenty-eight. She has real Nuradyne loyalty — she believes, or believed,
in the dome system, in the job of keeping fourteen million people breathing — which is exactly why
she can't let go of what she suspects about how the reactors actually work. She runs her chief-of-
security job by the book in public and keeps a second, unofficial file in private: every "abnormal
reading" incident Harmon buried, cross-referenced against who died, transferred, or went quiet
afterward. She has one live source feeding that file: a broken, terrified old man in the slums she has
been protecting off the record for three years, without either of them using the word "protecting."

**Feodor Kessler — the stranded liaison.** Canon-unchanged in origin: FMA liaison stranded by the war,
traded multiversal knowledge to Nuradyne for years of protection, cut loose once the engineers no
longer needed him. New here: Voight found him eleven months after he was cut loose, during a routine
security sweep of slum-district "persons of interest" nobody upstairs cared enough to close out. She
recognized his name from a flagged internal memo Season had once shown her — a memo Harmon's people
never fully scrubbed — and instead of filing the standard disposal recommendation, she quietly told
Dutch Marsh to make sure nothing happened to him, in exchange for whatever he'd tell her. Kessler
doesn't know what the FMA actually is beyond the word itself and a portal he never got to use again.
Voight doesn't know what the FMA is either. She knows Nuradyne's reactors aren't ordinary reactors,
that Season died for getting close to that fact, and that Kessler is the only living person outside
Nuradyne's inner circle who can say why.

**Director Harmon.** Canon-unchanged: head of the reactor project, stonewalls Ryker, suppressed the
abnormal-reactor reports. New here: Harmon was the one who ordered Season's "containment failure."
He has never said so to Voight, and she has never accused him — the entire relationship between them
for eight years has been conducted in that unspoken space. He promoted her, in fact, partly because a
security chief who suspects and says nothing is safer than one who doesn't suspect at all and might
stumble into it blind.

**Threnody Vask — the operative.** From a drained universe roughly forty years further into its own
siphon damage than U-7134 would be if nothing changes: failing biosphere, collapsing agriculture,
maybe two generations of livable time left if the drain stops today. Vask isn't a soldier sent to
punish Quinmore. She's an engineer sent to *fix the meter* — her mission, worked out over a decade by
what's left of her world's scientific establishment, is to force a short, violent, one-time reversal
of the siphon: pull a catastrophic pulse of energy back across the aperture before it collapses, enough
to buy her people another generation, at the cost of blacking out a significant fraction of Quinmore's
power grid for months. Not enough to kill the whole city. Enough to kill people, in the parts of the
city too poor to matter to the CU board's timeline for restoring them. She has done the math. She
thinks it's a fair trade for what's already been taken from her. She isn't sure she's right, and that
uncertainty is the whole reason she's willing to talk to Ryker instead of just finishing the job.

**Dutch Marsh.** Slum broker. Takes Nuradyne security-budget money to keep half an eye on Kessler
without asking why, because Voight pays better than her curiosity costs.

**Priya Okonkwo-Lindqvist.** CU board fixer, dispatched once the relocation-warehouse story starts
generating the wrong kind of press. Wants the incident closed, the district reopened, and nobody's
name in a headline. Doesn't know about the multiverse and doesn't want to.

**Ryker's FMA partner (unnamed handler, "Six").** Stays at the outpost, feeds objectives by infolink.
Warm in tone, careful in word choice — the kind of careful that Ryker doesn't clock as strange until
he has a reason to go back and reread every message.

## Full plot, mission by mission

### Act 1 — What the FMA sent him to find

**FMA HQ, recall and briefing.** Unchanged from canon: medical, armory, optional orientation. The
briefing officer is explicit that this is assessment, not intervention — "you are not going in to fix
anything, you are going in to tell us what's there." Six is assigned as his infolink partner, warm,
competent, reassuring. Nothing here reads as false. That's the point; it isn't false, it's just
incomplete.

**Outpost, triangulation, Northgate, the train.** Unchanged from canon. In Northgate, thugs jump
Ryker — first combat or stealth choice of the game, low stakes, teaches the toolkit. One thug, if
taken alive and questioned, mentions almost nothing about Nuradyne and everything about the checkpoint
lines choking off half the district's smuggling routes since "the bombing" — first mention, secondhand
and distorted, of what's actually happened downtown.

**Slum district entrance.** Unchanged: the mother, the Nuradyne officer, Ryker's forged clearance, the
mother's plea to find her son. Small addition: as Ryker's badge scans through, the checkpoint log
briefly flags for a supervisor review — Voight's name is visible on the terminal as the officer
clears it manually rather than escalate, because escalating means paperwork and paperwork means
Harmon's office notices a name that shouldn't have clearance. First glimpse of Voight before the
player has met her: a name on a screen, not yet a person, already quietly making a call that keeps
the story moving without her superiors knowing why.

**Reactor investigation.** Unchanged: erased logs, dead security, the vent datacube naming Harmon and
"abnormal reactor readings." The scene adds one more find, easy to miss: a second, older log fragment
in the reactor control room's local terminal — not erased, because nobody with the authority to erase
it thought to check a system this low-priority — timestamped eleven months earlier, referencing a
prior "Season Incident" and a security sign-off closing it as equipment failure. Reading it doesn't
trigger anything yet. It's a name Ryker will need later.

**Finding Harmon.** Unchanged: rehearsed answers, dismissal of the datacube, stonewalling. New beat:
Harmon mentions, almost as an aside meant to close the conversation, that "Chief Voight handles
personnel matters relating to the incident" — trying to hand Ryker off to someone he assumes will be
a wall, not a door.

### Act 2 — Aire Gardens, the kid, and the woman running her own file

**Aire Gardens.** The multiport level from `Ideas.md`: the luxury train-station-and-landing-pad
complex Ryker passes through en route to Nuradyne's deeper security infrastructure, glossy ads for
dome-life plastered over concourses two checkpoints away from the relocation warehouse. The elevator
down to the subway level, taken in-level rather than as a load transition, gives the player the one
sanctioned look outside the glass on the way down — filtered daylight over a dead grey horizon,
gone in ninety seconds. Ryker isn't here for the view; he's here because Nuradyne's holding facility
for "detained persons of interest" from the sealed district sits at the bottom of this transit spine,
and the missing kid is in it.

**The missing kid.** Unchanged: found in an unlogged holding cell, scared, half-forgotten. New content
in what he says. He tells Ryker (converse) that the woman who found him — "the one with the silver
badge, not the loud ones" — was the one who told the guards to log him as a transfer instead of
leaving him off the books entirely, and told them, in front of him, that if anyone touched him she'd
know. He doesn't understand it as protection. He describes it as the scariest person in the building
being scared of someone else. This is the player's first real information about Voight, delivered
entirely through the kid's own confused read on an adult conversation — not proof of anything, just
the first crack in "company loyalist."

The kid also mentions, offhand, something he overheard while detained: two guards discussing "the
readings again, just like the old one" and a name — Season — that means nothing to him and everything,
soon, to Ryker.

**First contact with Voight.** Ryker's forged credentials get flagged at the security-division level
inside Nuradyne HQ — not by an automated system, by Voight personally reviewing checkpoint anomalies,
because that's exactly the kind of loose thread her job requires her to pull. She has him brought in
for questioning, not arrest: professional, controlled, reading him carefully rather than threatening
him. This is the game's first real Voight scene and it's built to run several ways depending on player
approach — Ryker can stonewall her the way Harmon stonewalled him (she recognizes the tactic
immediately and respects it, which unsettles her more than a bad liar would), can offer a partial,
plausible cover story, or can gamble and mention the Season Incident by name.

Mentioning Season is the hinge. She doesn't confirm anything in the room — she has spent eight years
not confirming anything in rooms — but something in her stops being performance. She lets Ryker go
with a warning that reads, correctly, as a woman buying herself time to think rather than a woman
closing a file.

**The liaison, found via Voight's own trail.** Reading a terminal in Voight's security-division wing
(a computer-read reveal, in-verb) turns up an unremarkable-looking expense authorization: a standing
"informant stipend," routed through Dutch Marsh's known accounts, renewed annually for three years,
categorized under a budget line general enough not to draw attention. It triggers an infolink from
Six — the FMA has been monitoring financial patterns in the district and this one matches a person of
interest they lost track of years ago: Feodor Kessler. Ryker's next objective, self-directed rather
than handed to him wholesale: find Marsh, find Kessler.

**Marsh and the slums.** Marsh deals, cagily, once Ryker demonstrates he already knows more than a
stranger should — about Voight's money, about Kessler's name. He won't betray the arrangement outright
(Voight pays too well and Marsh has survived this long by not burning quiet, reliable clients) but he
can be persuaded, bought, or pressured into pointing Ryker at Kessler's building, on the understanding
that Ryker didn't hear Marsh's name from Marsh.

**Kessler.** A frightened, aging man in a single sealed room who has spent three years waiting for
either Nuradyne or something worse to finish what they started. Conversing with him — carefully, the
scene is built to punish a heavy hand — gets the real dump: what the FMA is, what U-7134's reactors
actually run on, what he traded and to whom, and the detail that changes the shape of the whole
investigation for Ryker: he didn't only trade knowledge to Harmon's engineers. Years ago, once he
realized the arrangement with Nuradyne's inner circle might not protect him forever, he told a junior
security officer — new to the job, sharp, scared of nothing — enough of the truth to make her start
asking questions of her own. He never learned what came of it. He assumes, correctly, that it's the
reason Chief Voight has quietly kept him alive ever since without either of them naming the debt.

This is the scene where the player learns, explicitly and from the source, that **Voight has known
something was deeply wrong with the reactors for years** — not the full multiversal truth, which
Kessler never gave her in plain terms, but enough to know Harmon was hiding something worth killing
Season over, and to have spent three years quietly protecting the one man who could eventually prove
it.

**Confronting Voight, round two — the recruitment scene.** With Kessler's testimony and the Season
file in hand, Ryker can go back to Voight and lay it out. This is the game's central persuasion
set-piece and the first real branch point: Ryker can threaten her with exposure (blackmail — she has
more to lose than anyone in the building and knows it), can appeal to what got her into this in the
first place (Season, the fourteen million people the domes are nominally protecting), or can simply
tell her the truth about the FMA and let her decide what to do with knowing it's real. Any of these
can flip her; the tone of the alliance that results is different depending which one the player uses,
and that tone carries into the endings. A player who threatens her gets a Voight who helps out of
leverage and resents it. A player who's straight with her gets a Voight who becomes, cautiously, an
actual ally — sharing files, running interference with her own department, buying Ryker time inside
Nuradyne HQ he couldn't get any other way. A player who never approaches her at all leaves her running
her own parallel track for the rest of the game, sometimes helpful by accident, sometimes an obstacle.

**The operative surfaces.** Independent of the Voight thread, Ryker's investigation into the reactor
site turns up evidence — witness accounts from surviving reactor-floor staff, a partial thermal
signature Nuradyne's own forensics team logged and buried — that whoever caused the explosion is still
in the district, and isn't hiding, she's *working*: moving through service tunnels toward the Aire
Gardens subway relay that routes backup power to three downtream dome sectors. Reading a Nuradyne
forensics terminal (computer-read reveal) triggers an infolink from Six with a new objective:
intercept before the relay is compromised.

**First encounter with Vask.** Not a boss fight by default — the scene is built so a purely violent
approach is possible but throws away content. Approached carefully, or if Ryker holds fire in the
opening seconds, Vask will talk (converse-triggered): she identifies herself, states plainly that she
isn't here to hurt Quinmore's people any more than the job requires, and that the job requires this
relay. She doesn't explain the "correction" in full yet — she's sizing Ryker up the same way he's
sizing her up. She leaves before Ryker can act on whatever he decides, having planted a device at the
relay that isn't triggered yet. This is the scene that turns "operative" into a person with a
stated, comprehensible reason for what she's doing, ahead of the plot's second twist.

### Act 3 — Two twists, in order

**Twist one: the FMA's real orders.** Back at the outpost thread, an infolink exchange with Six —
routine, following up on the Kessler find — goes slightly wrong. Six asks Ryker to prioritize
retrieving physical reactor schematics and, specifically, any documentation of *how* Nuradyne
achieved stable siphon output, ahead of neutralizing the immediate threat. Phrased as due diligence.
If Ryker pushes back or asks why assessment now needs hardware, Six's answers get careful in a way
they weren't before. A follow-up infolink — this one clearly not meant for Ryker, forwarded by
accident or surfaced through a terminal in the outpost's own systems if the player goes looking —
makes it explicit: FMA leadership has been aware for longer than they told Ryker that siphon
technology, properly controlled, could resolve the FMA's own long-term energy problem across its vast
multiversal infrastructure. The "assessment" mission was never just assessment. Extraction was the
real objective from the start; Ryker was the plausible-deniability version of the team they'd send if
extraction alone were the stated goal. This lands with the same shape as the canon's own invitation —
does the FMA have a hidden agenda? — but the agenda here is institutional appetite, not a licensing
regime deciding who's allowed to be drained. The FMA polices exactly this crime everywhere else and
wants a supervised version of it for itself.

**Twist two: Vask's correction.** Whether or not Ryker took Vask's earlier offer to talk, the plot
forces a second meeting once the relay device's timer becomes active — Six flags a massive power
spike building at the Aire Gardens junction, and Voight (if allied) flags the same thing from inside
Nuradyne, independently, because her security division is watching the same grid. Cornered or
approached again, Vask lays out the full plan: the device isn't a bomb. It's a resonance driver, built
from Nuradyne's own reactor telemetry that her people intercepted for a decade, designed to force a
brief, violent reopening of the aperture and pull a massive one-time energy pulse back across it —
enough to buy her dying universe another generation, at the cost of blacking out a large fraction of
Quinmore's grid, concentrated in the sectors with the oldest infrastructure, for months. Not the whole
city. Not everyone. Enough people, in the parts of the city the CU board already treats as
disposable, that it amounts to a second slum-district catastrophe, deliberately aimed. She isn't
gloating about it. She states, flatly, that she has run every other option her world's scientists
could find over ten years and this is what's left, and that she is not fully certain she's right to do
it, which is the only reason Ryker is still breathing after she's told him this much.

This is the twist that cashes the brief's "multiverse-siphon horror" requirement directly: the
audience has spent the whole game horrified on behalf of the drained universes in the abstract, and
this scene forces that horror to have a face, a plan, and an argument, aimed at a specific and
familiar kind of victim — the poor half of the very city the player has been walking through.

### The convergence

With both twists live, the endgame is a triangle: the FMA wants the reactor's secrets extracted
before anything else happens to the site; Vask wants to complete the correction; Nuradyne (Harmon,
and the inner circle behind him) wants the whole thing contained, quietly, permanently, which by now
plausibly includes Ryker, Voight, and Kessler on a very short list of loose ends. Voight, if allied,
independently reaches the same conclusion Ryker does about Harmon's endgame — she finds, in her own
building, a draft containment order with her own name on the "personnel review" line, dated for after
whatever happens next resolves. Harmon has decided she's a liability regardless of how this ends. It's
the first time in eight years the unspoken threat that's shaped her whole career gets put in writing,
and it's the scene, if she's still an ally, where she stops hedging.

The endgame space (Nuradyne HQ reactor control, the Aire Gardens relay, and a final confrontation that
can happen in either location depending on player pacing) supports the following moves, all of them
reachable through converse, infolink, incapacitation, multitool access, or terminal reads, with no
surveillance or timestamp-hacking required anywhere in the chain:

- Ryker can side with the FMA's extraction order, securing the schematics for Six's team before
  anything else is resolved.
- Ryker can help Vask complete or abort the correction.
- Ryker can move against Harmon directly, using whatever combination of Voight's internal authority,
  Kessler's testimony, and physical evidence the player has gathered.
- Ryker can try to thread more than one of the above, at a real cost — the game does not let every
  thread resolve cleanly if the player runs out of time balancing all three.

## Endings

**1. The Correction.** Ryker helps Vask complete the reversal. The Aire Gardens relay and several
downstream sectors go permanently dark; evacuation is chaotic, deaths are real and reported, and the
CU board's cover story ("a second attack, same terrorists") holds because nobody outside Nuradyne's
inner circle, the FMA, and whoever Ryker told knows the truth well enough to contradict it. Vask's
universe gets its generation. Voight, if allied, is the one who has to manage the evacuation from
inside Nuradyne and lives the rest of her career carrying the knowledge that she helped choose who
paid for it. Thematically the purest expression of Ryker's stated ethic — ends justify the means —
turned all the way up until the means are Quinmore's own poor.

**2. The Extraction.** Ryker sides with the FMA, secures Harmon's reactor documentation (through
Voight's access if allied, through force if not), and stops Vask by any means necessary — including
lethal, if the player chooses — before the correction fires. Six's team quietly extracts everything.
The FMA leaves U-7134 with what it came for and Ryker gets a commendation and no straight answer about
what happens to the tech next. Nuradyne's siphon keeps running exactly as it did before; nothing about
Quinmore changes; the only difference is that a second institution now has the knowledge, unaccountable
to anyone Ryker will ever meet again. The bleakest ending, and the one that most directly indicts the
FMA rather than Nuradyne.

**3. The Exposure.** Ryker and Voight, if she's still allied by this point, go public — Kessler's
testimony, Season's real cause of death, the reactor telemetry, routed to Okonkwo-Lindqvist and the CU
board in a form too documented to bury, timed so it breaks before Harmon can move on either of them.
The siphon reactors get shut down pending "safety review" — a euphemism, because nobody in the CU is
willing to say the real word in public. Large parts of Quinmore lose power in a slower, more managed
version of what the Correction ending does by force; the dome system goes into rationed brownout for
months while alternative generation is scrambled together. Harmon is arrested or disappears depending
on player choices earlier. Voight loses her career, possibly her freedom, and gains the thing she's
wanted since Season died: the truth on the record. Vask's universe gets nothing — the reactors are
shut down too late and too locally to matter to her people, and if the player never resolved her
thread directly, she's left to try the correction alone, off-screen, unresolved. The ending that costs
the most in the short term and is the only one that actually stops the crime at its source.

**4. The Compromise.** Ryker helps Voight move against Harmon specifically — not the reactors, not the
whole apparatus, just the man — using leverage rather than exposure: Season's real cause of death,
Kessler's testimony, and (if the player pursued it) direct evidence of the containment order with her
name on it, enough to force Harmon out and instate Voight as the one holding the reactor program's
real secret. The siphon keeps running. Nobody outside the inner circle ever learns the truth. What
changes is who's making the call about how hard to push it, and Voight — who has spent eight years
being the person in the room who knew and said nothing — becomes, by the ending, exactly that person
by design instead of by cowardice, with the added weight of knowing it and choosing it anyway. The
most morally uncomfortable ending: nothing is fixed, nobody outside is protected, and the story's most
sympathetic figure ends it running the machine she spent the whole game trying to expose, on the
theory that someone with a conscience running it is better than Harmon. The game does not confirm
she's right.

Each ending is reachable without ever fighting Vask to the death and without ever fully trusting or
fully betraying Voight — the branch points are about who Ryker tells what, and when, not about combat
outcomes, in keeping with the game's stated commitment to converse/infolink/kill-or-incapacitate/
multitool/read-computer as the full reveal toolkit.

## Themes

**Complicity has a face before it has a policy.** The game spends its first half asking who's guilty
of an abstraction — a reactor, a corporation, an authority — and its second half making the player
look at Voight, Vask, and Kessler, three people who each did something defensible for reasons that
made sense to them, and add up to a working atrocity anyway.

**Knowing and choosing not to act is its own decision, repeated daily.** Voight's arc is built entirely
around the gap between suspicion and proof, and the story's argument is that she has been making a
choice every single day for eight years by not closing that gap sooner, whether or not she'd call it
a choice.

**Institutions protect institutions first, including the ones sent to police them.** Nuradyne buries
its own scientist; the CU board wants a boring headline more than the truth; the FMA, established here
as the story's ostensible white hat, turns out to want the crime's fruits rather than its end. No
faction in this story is exempt from the thing it exists to prevent in someone else.

**"Ends justify the means" only stays comfortable at a distance.** Ryker's stated ethic is tested
directly by Vask's correction — the exact math he's been running informally on every mission (acceptable
losses, necessary evils) done explicitly, in front of him, by someone asking him to help carry it out
against people who look like the mother from the checkpoint rather than an anonymous enemy.
