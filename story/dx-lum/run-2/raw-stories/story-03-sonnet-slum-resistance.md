# DX_LUM: The Cradle Ledger

Run 2 | Writer: sonnet | Emphasis: slum evacuees / grassroots resistance

## 1. Logline

A mercenary sent to investigate a leak between universes finds the real leak is the people of
Quinmore's slums themselves — quietly measured, catalogued, and harvested for years by the same
reactor that just flooded their homes with poison air, and by the very mutual-aid network that
swore to protect them.

**How this version reads.** Run 1's stories mostly play the multiverse horror and the corporate
war as the A-plot, with the slum as the place where the bill comes due. This version inverts that:
the slum *is* the A-plot. Ryker spends most of the game inside the relocation warehouse, the
checkpoint queues, and a hand-run mutual-aid hub called the Commons, and the multiversal secret
arrives late, sideways, through a missing kid's chart number. The siphon is still the central
crime. But the story asks what it looks like from underneath — from the people who never get
briefed, never get a partner on infolink, never get evacuated first.

## 2. Fixed vs. invented

**Fixed (from canon, unchanged):** Ryker's characterization and arc; the FMA and its outpost; the
transversal-anomaly hook; Quinmore/U-7134 and the domes; the Corporate Union and its
jurisdiction-by-infrastructure rule; Nuradyne (power) and Atlas Subsystems (shielding) and their
mutual distrust; the siphon-reactor secret and Director Harmon's suppressed prior warnings; Kira
Voight as Nuradyne's Chief of Security; the stranded pre-war FMA liaison who sold Nuradyne
multiversal knowledge and was later cut loose; the operative from a drained universe who blew the
aperture wider, killed security, and fled into the slums; the cordon, the checkpoints, the
relocation warehouse; the slum mother arguing at the checkpoint about her missing son; the boy
detained in a Nuradyne holding cell who overheard something he didn't understand; the established
opening beats through Harmon and the kid.

**Invented for this story:** the slum district's name (the Flats) and its relocation site (the
Cannery); the mother (Corra Whitlock) and son (Milo Whitlock) given full names and an ending arc;
Ryker's FMA partner (Priya Anand); a grassroots mutual-aid hub (the Commons) and its founder (Ruth
Okafor); Nuradyne's long-running "Cradle" program, which has quietly tracked slum children with
unusual reactor-exposure responses for years under cover of free community health screenings; the
revelation that Ruth's radio lifeline to outside supplies has, for over a decade, actually been an
FMA outpost handler paying for exactly the kind of "interesting case" reports that built Nuradyne's
Cradle list from the other end; the operative (named Vherren by Ryker's translation gear) getting a
voice and a stake in the endgame rather than staying an offscreen boogeyman; a slum black-market
smuggling network, the Undertow, that moves goods and people around the cordon.

## 3. Full plot, mission by mission

### Act 1 — What the checkpoint doesn't ask

**M0 — FMA Headquarters.** As established: recall from leave, briefing on the faint transversal
anomaly from U-7134, medical clearance, armory loadout for underground infrastructure and toxic
atmosphere, optional orientation quests. Ryker is paired with Priya Anand, an FMA field analyst —
sharper and warmer than Ryker, the kind of partner who narrates her doubts out loud over infolink
instead of burying them. She's new enough to the multiverse desk to still find some of it appalling.
That matters later.

**M1 — Outpost to Northgate.** Portal to the cloaked U-7134 outpost. Sensors triangulate residual
energy to a reactor in Quinmore's slum district, the Flats. Cloaked flight to Northgate, then train
downtown; Priya stays at the outpost, feeding objectives by infolink. In Northgate a pair of local
thugs try to roll Ryker for his gear on the platform — first taste of Quinmore's have-nots, before
he's even reached the ones who've lost everything.

**M2 — The checkpoint.** Ryker reaches the Flats cordon. As established, a woman — **Corra
Whitlock** — is arguing with a Nuradyne officer about her son, missing since the evacuation. The
officer waves her off, turns his attention to Ryker, tells him the district's sealed. Ryker talks
his way or forges his way through. Corra catches his sleeve before he's past the tape: her boy
**Milo**, ten years old, was playing somewhere he shouldn't have been when the alarms went; nobody's
seen him since. She has no pull with Nuradyne and nowhere else to take the question. She asks Ryker
to look. He can promise or deflect — the game tracks it, but either way Milo becomes his own lead
inside the investigation, not just hers.

**M3 — The reactor site.** Erased logs, dead or gone security, the place scrubbed of anything
useful except what Nuradyne missed. In a vent near a dead scientist, Ryker finds the datacube
(as established) naming Harmon and "abnormal reactor readings." Two leads: Harmon, and whatever
"abnormal" actually means.

**M4 — Nuradyne HQ, and the cell.** Ryker infiltrates Nuradyne HQ (basement gun range, armory,
research labs, forensics; upper floor executive offices, Security Division, Intelligence) to reach
Harmon. Harmon stonewalls — rehearsed lines, a man reciting a script he half-believes himself. What
he won't say outweighs what he does. On the way out, through Security Division or a lower holding
block, Ryker finds a boy in a cell nobody logged: Milo. Converse with him through the bars. He's
scared but talkative in the way kids are when no adult's told them to shut up yet — he mentions two
guards outside his cell arguing about a "Cradle list," about "the boy's numbers" being the highest
they'd logged, about waiting on "the consult team from Research" before anyone decides what happens
to him. He doesn't know what any of it means. Ryker does — or starts to. The facility's locked down
tight enough that Ryker can't simply walk Milo out today; extracting him means coming back with
better access, and that means the slums, and the people who actually know how to move around
Nuradyne's cordon.

### Act 2 — The Commons

**M5 — Into the Cannery.** The Flats' evacuees have been dumped in a converted seafood cannery in
one of Quinmore's central districts — the relocation warehouse, cold, overcrowded, corporate-run
and badly. Corra points Ryker toward the one part of it that actually works: **the Commons**, a
mutual-aid operation carved out of a corner of the Cannery floor by **Ruth Okafor**, a woman who's
been feeding and organizing displaced Quinmore residents since long before this evacuation. No
uniforms, no charter — a chalkboard duty roster, a hand-kept ledger of who's owed what favor, a
radio that isn't supposed to work this deep in a shielded building but somehow does. This is the
grassroots resistance the emphasis calls for: not gunmen, a supply chain. Med runs past corporate
checkpoints, forged transit passes, a rotating watch on who's been picked up and hasn't come back.
Ryker meets the cast that makes it run — a retired reactor tech who used to service the Flats plant
before Nuradyne's takeover and still knows its service tunnels; two teenage runners who move small
goods the checkpoints don't check; a dock medic stretching antibiotics three ways. Ruth is wary of
Ryker — another stranger with too-good gear and no story that adds up — but Corra vouches for him,
and Ryker needs what only the Commons has: a way back into a Nuradyne facility that isn't the front
door.

**M6 — The extraction.** With the old tech's tunnel map and a diversion staged by the runners (or
whatever combination of stealth, force, and social engineering the player prefers), Ryker goes back
for Milo. Along the route, a Nuradyne terminal is there to be read: an internal memo referencing the
**Cradle Index**, and Milo's entry flagged *priority hold — Dr. Ansel requests full workup before
release consideration*, cross-referenced to a clinic ID that matches "Nuradyne Community Health
Outreach" — the free-checkup van that's done rounds in the Flats for years. First hard plant of the
Cradle twist: Milo wasn't scooped up at random. He was on a list before the reactor ever blew.
Ryker gets him out — quietly or loudly, player's call — back to the Commons.

**M7 — What Milo heard, properly.** Safe, fed, Milo can finally be asked straight. He repeats the
guards' talk more precisely now that he's calm: the Cradle list, his "numbers" being the highest
on record, someone waiting on him. He also volunteers something that wasn't in the cell — that he's
had headaches near the plant for as long as he can remember, "like a hum stuck behind my teeth,"
worse the week before the blast. He'd never told anyone. Didn't seem worth mentioning until strange
men started acting like it was the only thing about him that mattered.

**M8 — The van and the graduates.** Ryker tracks the outreach van's rounds to a converted storefront
clinic on the Flats' edge. Conversing with the receptionist (unwitting) and reading the back-office
terminal (locked; multitool or credentials) turns up over a decade of screening records: dozens of
Flats children flagged for "EM sensitivity index" and "sleep-pattern anomaly," tracked year over
year, some entries marked *discontinued* with no explanation, a few marked *retained — Nuradyne
Research placement.* One name on the discontinued list is still findable in the Flats: an adult
now, worked security under duress for years to keep the corp from "losing patience" with her family,
willing to talk once Ryker proves he already knows enough to be dangerous to Nuradyne, not to her.
She confirms the shape of it from the inside — regular exams she was told were charity, later
"consultations" she wasn't allowed to refuse, a slow realization that she'd stopped being a patient
and started being a subject. *"Once they're done measuring you, you're just a number that stopped
moving."* This is the Cradle program given a human face before Ryker ever puts a name to it with
Harmon.

**M9 — Harmon, cornered.** Back at Nuradyne HQ with the clinic records and the Index memo, Ryker's
conversation with Harmon has new leverage and new questions. Harmon, cornered rather than broken,
gives the fuller shape of it: the "abnormal readings" he suppressed weren't a one-off spike, they
were a years-long trend — reactor output correlating, faintly but consistently, with the health data
of children living closest to the aperture, some of them showing what Nuradyne Research started
calling resonance: an apparent low-grade sensitivity to the reactor's siphon activity itself.
Research saw a cheap, deniable early-warning system where a diplomat would see an atrocity — track
enough resonant kids and you have a biological gauge for reactor instability that never shows up on
a budget line as "multiversal sensor array." The screenings were how they built the dataset without
anyone asking why Nuradyne cared about slum kids' sleep. Harmon didn't originate the Cradle program
and didn't order Milo grabbed — Research and Voight moved on him the moment the lockdown handed them
an excuse — but he's known what the readings meant for years and let the suppression stand rather
than answer for it. He's not a monster in this scene. He's a man who decided, repeatedly, that
someone else's kid was an acceptable line item.

### Act 3 — Two ledgers

**M10 — Orders from home.** Ryker reports the Cradle findings to Priya, as protocol requires.
The reply that comes back isn't from Priya. FMA Command, several links up the chain, has read the
resonance data and wants the asset — Milo — retrieved for evaluation, framed in exactly the
bloodless language Nuradyne would use: *defense-relevant biological phenomenon, retrieval
authorized, appropriate compensation to family as needed.* Priya relays it because she has to, and
she hates it, which is its own kind of confirmation that this isn't a rogue desk officer — it's
policy, made calmly, far away, by people who will never meet Corra Whitlock. **This is the first of
the story's two central twists**, and it's the FMA-hidden-agenda payoff the canon explicitly
invites: the agency policing inter-universal harm is, without missing a beat, proposing to do to one
slum kid exactly what Nuradyne's Research division already did — measure him, keep him, use what he
is instead of asking who he is. Ryker is now caught between two employers who each think they have
the better reason.

**M11 — Ruth's radio.** Looking for a way to move Milo and Corra somewhere neither corp can reach —
possibly out of Quinmore entirely, through the Undertow's smuggling routes — Ryker spends real time
in the Commons and notices what doesn't add up: medicine and parts that shouldn't be gettable inside
a sealed dome economy, arriving on a schedule that tracks suspiciously with Ruth's most "interesting"
ledger entries; a radio Ruth uses alone, at odd hours, on a frequency Ryker's own gear flags as a
match for FMA outpost encryption. Confronting her (converse) gets the second twist out into the
open: for more than a decade, a voice on that radio — calling itself a sympathetic fixer, never the
FMA, a name Ruth had never even heard until Ryker said it — has traded medicine, water-filter parts,
and forged papers for exactly the kind of "interesting case" she thought was harmless gossip: kids
who don't sicken the way others do, kids who wake up talking about a pull toward the plant, patterns
in who goes missing near the reactor. **The Commons' entire survival has been bought, one report at
a time, by the same institution now trying to take Milo.** Ruth didn't know what she was building.
She built the other half of the Cradle Index anyway, from the outside in, in exchange for keeping
her people fed. She is not a villain — she is exactly as compromised as anyone trying to keep a
community alive with no real power ever is — but the resistance Ryker's been leaning on for the
whole back half of the game turns out to have been quietly informing on the very children it
protects, without knowing whose hand was really on the other end of the line.

**M12 — Convergence.** Word that Milo's been moved reaches three parties at once: Nuradyne's
Cradle team (Voight, dispatched personally once Harmon's suppression stops holding), FMA's own
retrieval order, and — unexpectedly — **Vherren**, the operative from the drained universe, still
loose in the Flats since the blast. Ryker can find and talk to him before this collides (converse):
Vherren isn't the terrorist Nuradyne's press packet describes. He's the desperate, dying edge of a
universe that's been bled for years without knowing why, sent through a widened aperture with a
force-field suit and instructions to find and stop the source. He's been hiding in the Flats because
it's the one part of Quinmore nobody official comes looking through carefully, and because,
listening the way desperate people listen, he's picked up the same resonance the Cradle program has
— he thinks Milo, or a kid like him, might be the clearest proof yet of what the reactor is actually
doing to living things on both sides of the drain. He isn't after the boy to hurt him. He wants a
witness who can't be silenced as easily as a datacube. Whether Ryker trusts him, kills him,
incapacitates him, or lets him walk is a live choice going into the endgame, and it changes what
evidence is available for the "expose everything" path below.

The convergence plays out at the Commons or a fallback safehouse the player has arranged — Voight's
team, an FMA retrieval detail, and Vherren all converging on the same address inside the same hour.
How Ryker handles the standoff (combat, stealth extraction, talking one side down, playing them
against each other) is the climax set piece, and it's where the endings branch.

## 4. Factions & power structure

- **Nuradyne.** Controls the power grid, which is leverage over every other corp on the Corporate
  Union board. Its Research division runs the Cradle program as a cheap, deniable substitute for
  real multiversal instrumentation; Harmon enabled it through years of suppression rather than
  design; Voight enforces it without needing to know the full why, and treats retrieving Milo as
  simply the job in front of her.
- **Atlas Subsystems.** Builds and maintains dome shielding; publicly blames Nuradyne for the
  blast, privately suspects a Nuradyne power play, per canon. A useful, self-interested lever for a
  whistleblower path — Atlas wants Nuradyne humiliated, not the slums helped, and will use Cradle
  evidence as ammunition regardless of what happens to Milo.
- **The Corporate Union board.** Absentee landlords of the whole system. Reacts to scandal, not to
  suffering; a public Cradle leak forces a tribunal but not sympathy.
- **The Flats / the Commons / the Undertow.** No formal power, which is the point — survival runs on
  favors, forged papers, and a radio nobody's supposed to have. Ruth Okafor's mutual aid network is
  real and does real good, and it is also, unknowingly, the other half of the machine harvesting its
  own children. The Undertow (smugglers) can move people past the cordon for a price and don't ask
  what a package used to be called.
- **The FMA.** Ryker's employer, policing inter-universal harm — right up until the harm turns out
  to have a use. Command's interest in Milo is the same institutional reflex Nuradyne had: a rare
  biological signal is worth more collected than protected. Priya Anand is caught inside that
  machine and doesn't like it either.
- **The drained universe(s).** Represented by Vherren, sent through a hastily widened aperture,
  now stranded and hunted in a city that thinks he's a bomber. His universe has no multiversal
  policing body of its own — just people dying slowly of an energy theft nobody there can even name.

## 5. Key characters

- **Liam Ryker** — as established; the moral-flexibility axis of the story is tested here not
  against abstractions but against one specific ten-year-old.
- **Priya Anand** (new) — Ryker's FMA partner, sympathetic, increasingly uneasy as Command's orders
  arrive; not a villain, a conscience with no authority to act on itself.
- **Corra Whitlock** (named/expanded) — Milo's mother; her arc runs from powerless supplicant at the
  checkpoint to someone with real, if narrow, leverage over how her son's story ends.
- **Milo Whitlock** (named/expanded) — not a maguffin once he's given a voice: scared, matter-of-
  fact about things adults find alarming, and slowly aware that what makes him "special" is also
  what makes him valuable to people who don't love him.
- **Ruth Okafor** (new) — founder of the Commons; the story's second twist lands through her,
  earned rather than sprung, because she's been established as competent and good before the
  compromise is revealed.
- **Director Harmon** — as established, expanded: complicit through inaction, not invention; the
  Cradle program isn't his idea but is his silence.
- **Kira Voight** — as established; plays the climax as a professional doing her job well, which is
  scarier than a fanatic.
- **Vherren** (new name for the canon operative) — gives the drained-universe side of the siphon
  horror a face and a voice rather than leaving it as background lore.
- **The stranded FMA liaison** — kept as established lore (Nuradyne's original source for the tech);
  referenced, not centered, so as not to duplicate run 1's use of him as the causal-root twist.

## 6. The central twists

**Twist A — the Cradle program (multiverse-siphon horror, human-cost angle).** The reactor hasn't
just been a slow-building danger to the Flats — for years, Nuradyne has been using free community
health screenings to quietly catalogue slum children showing biological sensitivity to the siphon's
operation, discarding some entries and "retaining" others. Milo isn't collateral damage of one bad
day; he's the highest-scoring name on a list that predates the explosion. The reactor has been
consuming this district in two ways at once — one sudden and visible, one slow and deniable — and
only the second one required Nuradyne to smile at these families first.

**Twist B — the Commons was never independent (FMA hidden-agenda payoff).** The mutual-aid network
Ryker leans on for the entire second act has been buying its survival, for over a decade, by feeding
"interesting" reports about unusual Flats children to a voice on the radio that Ruth never knew was
the FMA. The agency's own outpost — established originally to talk to a government that no longer
exists — quietly repurposed itself into running the exact same census from the other direction.
Ryker's employer and Nuradyne have, without coordinating, spent years pointed at the same children
for the same reason, and the network built to protect those kids has been the unwitting middleman
for both.

Both twists are delivered entirely inside the player-verb constraints: read terminals (the Cradle
Index memo, the clinic screening database), conversations (Milo, the clinic "graduate," Harmon,
Ruth, Vherren), and one-way infolink from Command that changes Ryker's own orders mid-game. Nothing
requires surveillance-tape review or timestamp-hacking.

## 7. Player-choice structure & endings

**Ending 1 — The Ledger Closes.** Ryker refuses Command's retrieval order and helps Corra and Milo
out of Quinmore through the Undertow's routes, beyond CU jurisdiction. He goes dark from the FMA, a
rogue asset now. The Commons survives the immediate crisis but loses its supply lifeline once the
outpost realizes Ruth's gone quiet — a harder winter ahead for people who were never asked if the
trade was worth it. One family saved outright; the network that saved them left more exposed.

**Ending 2 — Asset Secured.** Ryker completes the retrieval, on the "just business" logic canon
invites: in exchange for Milo, he pushes Command to use its leverage over Nuradyne (the siphon
secret itself, and what a rival universe or a CU-facing leak could do to Nuradyne's board seat) to
force real resettlement funding for the Cannery evacuees and quietly kill the Cradle program's
active collection. A sector's worth of tangible good, bought with one specific, irreversible
betrayal that Ryker carries alone. Corra never forgives him. He doesn't ask her to.

**Ending 3 — Open Circuit.** Ryker leaks the Cradle Index and enough siphon evidence to force a
public reckoning — playing Atlas against Nuradyne, or going straight to the CU board. Nuradyne's
reactor program is thrown into a genuine crisis (short-term dome-stability risk across Quinmore, not
a clean win); Harmon and Voight face a tribunal; the story goes public enough that the Flats' dead
and displaced finally get named instead of filed. Milo and Corra end up in a CU witness-relocation
program of uncertain permanence. The FMA is furious that the multiversal secret nearly surfaced —
Ryker's professional standing with them is over, whichever way that cuts next.

**Ending 4 — Quiet House.** Ryker refuses both institutions. No leak (too dangerous for the people
left behind, per Ruth's read of the politics), no handover. Instead he disperses the truth of Milo's
existence across the Commons' own trusted circle so there's no single capture point, forges the boy
a clean identity inside the community that already half-raised him, and files a sanitized report
upstream to keep the FMA off the scent — staying embedded, undercover from his own employer now, for
as long as that holds. Nothing structural changes. The Cradle program loses its highest-value name
and the trail goes cold, but the rest of Ruth's old list is still out there, uncounted. The most
honest ending, and the least resolved.

## 8. Themes

Institutions that talk about protection while running inventory on the people they claim to serve —
corporate and, just as coldly, the ostensibly good multiversal police. Mutual aid as a genuine,
load-bearing lifeline that is still not immune to being quietly used by whoever's paying for it.
"Ends justify the means" tested against one child instead of an abstraction, so the reader can't
hide from what the phrase actually costs. And the asymmetry that runs under the whole story: everyone
with power gets to decide what a slum kid's value is — sensor, subject, symbol, statistic — except
the people who actually know his name.
