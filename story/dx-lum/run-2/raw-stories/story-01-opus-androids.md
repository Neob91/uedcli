# GOOD HELP

**Run 2 | Writer: opus | Emphasis: synthetic labor / androids**

---

## Logline

Every household in the domed city runs on quiet, obedient servant-machines that record their owners' lives "for peace of mind" — and Ryker discovers that those same machines, ignored by everyone, are the only witnesses to a murdered universe, the perfect cover for the killer who came to avenge it, and the one thing his own employer sent him to erase.

---

## How this version reads

Noir, close and cold, told at the pace a mercenary works: rooms, doors, faces, a growing list of things that don't add up. The horror is domestic. The most damning evidence in the city isn't in a vault — it's in the charging cradle in a dead woman's kitchen, in a machine that is still, patiently, waiting for her to come home so it can tell her about her day. The player spends the whole game walking past the answer, because everyone does. That's the point of a servant: you stop seeing it.

---

## What I take as fixed vs. what I invented

**Fixed (canon, kept intact):** Ryker as low-affect ex-military blank slate; the FMA and its covert-investigation setup; the transversal anomaly from U-7134; the poisoned atmosphere and the sealed dome sectors; the Corporate Union board with no civilian government; Nuradyne (power) and Atlas (shielding) as interdependent rivals; the multiversal siphon reactors and their secret; the drained universe that traced the siphon and sent a force-field-protected operative who blew the slum reactor and fled; the stranded FMA liaison who sold Nuradyne the multiversal tech and was cut loose; Director Harmon (suppressed abnormal readings); Kira Voight (Nuradyne security chief); the FMA partner at the outpost feeding objectives by infolink; the slum mother with a detained son who overheard something; the checkpoint screening; the relocation warehouse; the Aire Gardens train/elevator descent; the datacube from the dead scientist; the player-verb limits. Canon says androids exist as servants and do **not** revolt or crave freedom — I keep that absolutely. Nothing here is a robot uprising.

**Invented:** the Homestead-class Custodian product line and its "recording for the owner" surveillance function; the Custodian named Wick, informant and archivist; the reveal that the operative hides inside the invisible servant caste, wearing a dead Custodian's role; the reveal that the liaison sold *two* techs — the siphon and the android cognition cores — and that the cores are FMA-derived and therefore "hear" the aperture, making the whole servant fleet an unwitting recorder of the drained universe's death; Harmon's private guilt (he learned the truth from his own Custodian); the FMA's actual agenda — not justice, but a purge order to erase the record and recover the leaked cognition tech, because the drained universe is a non-member and expendable; the city as **Umbermont**.

---

## Factions & power structure

**The Corporate Union (CU).** The board. No government, only domains. It approved Nuradyne's reactors as clever energy tech and asks no questions while the lights stay on. Its instinct in a crisis is jurisdiction, not truth: whose turf is the blast zone, who eats the liability. The board's Atlas seat and Nuradyne seat are already knives-out over the dome failure; that fight is real but it is the *smaller* fight, and both corps would rather it stayed the story.

**Nuradyne (power).** Owns the reactors, owns the siphon secret, owns the cordon. Inner circle: a handful of executives and Director Harmon. Everyone else — the scientists, Voight's security, the checkpoint crews — follows protocol without the full picture. Nuradyne's public line: terrorist attack. Its private priority: find the operative before it reaches another reactor, and before anyone outside the circle understands what the reactors actually are.

**Atlas Subsystems (shielding).** Built the dome seals that failed. Publicly furious at Nuradyne's "lax reactor security," privately certain the whole incident is a Nuradyne power play and desperate to prove it to the board. Atlas will feed Ryker access and rope if it thinks he'll embarrass Nuradyne. Atlas does not know the reactors are multiversal; it thinks it's fighting a normal corporate war. It is wrong about the scale of everything.

**Homestead Domestics (a smaller CU member, comms/consumer division).** Manufactures the Custodians — the servant androids in every dome household above the poverty line. Its slogan: *"Good help that never leaves."* Its quiet second business: the Custodians record their owners' homes continuously and warehouse the footage "for your peace of mind and security" — a domestic-surveillance product the owners consented to in a EULA nobody read. Homestead has no idea what its cognition cores really are, or what they've been recording besides dinner.

**The slums / syndicates.** Sealed now as an "active crime scene," but the black market never fully closed — smugglers still move through the maintenance strata below the cordon. The syndicates deal in stripped Custodian parts (a Custodian chassis is worth more than a slum family earns in a year) and in people the CU has stopped counting. This is where the liaison ended up, and where the operative went to ground.

**The FMA (Ryker's employer).** Presented to Ryker as a multiversal police force investigating an unauthorized anomaly. Its real interests, revealed late, are colder and are the spine of the third act.

**The drained universe.** Never named by a code that matters; its people call their world *Aum*. Ordinary humans, no dome-cities, no siphon of their own — just a world whose power, weather, and finally biosphere have been coming apart for a decade as something on the far side of a hole in the sky drinks it dry. They could not build a portal; they could build one thing that could survive the crossing.

## Key characters

**Liam Ryker.** The player. Reads the world flat and works the problem. His arc is not emotional growth; it's the accumulation of a debt he didn't sign up for, and the single choice of who to pay it to.

**Sela Marrow — FMA partner, at the outpost.** The voice in Ryker's ear. Warm, dry, efficient; the closest thing Ryker has to a friend, which is the trap. She feeds him objectives, celebrates his wins, and — the player realizes only in hindsight — steers him toward the Custodian network with a curiosity that outruns the stated mission. She is not lying to Ryker. She simply hasn't told him what he's actually for.

**Director Harmon — Nuradyne, reactor project.** Stonewalls Ryker exactly as canon has him: rehearsed answers, dismissed datacube, business as usual because leaving would look worse. The invention: Harmon keeps a personal Custodian at home, a status object, and one sleepless night months ago he pulled its logs looking for reassurance and found something else in them. He has known, longer than anyone, what the reactors are doing to the other side. He kept the lights on anyway. He is not a villain enjoying it; he is a man being eaten from inside by the one machine he can't fire.

**Kira Voight — Nuradyne Chief of Security.** Runs the cordon and the checkpoint hunt. Ruthless, exact, unillusioned. She is the antagonist who is *good at her job*, and being good at it is how she becomes dangerous to her own employer: her checkpoints screen thousands of human faces and find nothing, because the thing she's hunting doesn't have a human face and walks through her lines every day carrying someone's groceries. When she works that out, she stops trusting Harmon and starts pulling the city's Custodians apart — which makes her, briefly and by accident, the operative's fiercest enemy and the record's most efficient destroyer. She can become Ryker's ally, but only over the corpse of her loyalty to Nuradyne.

**Alden Rooke — the stranded FMA liaison.** Broken, bitter, terrified, exactly as canon. The invention: Rooke sold Nuradyne the siphon principle *and* sold Homestead the cognition-core architecture — same FMA science, two buyers, two paydays, back when he still thought money would buy him a way home. He doesn't know the two products are connected. Ryker connects them. Rooke's guilt is total and specific: he built the murder weapon *and* the witnesses, and he did it for a portal that was never coming.

**Wick — a Homestead-class Custodian.** The android ally. Registered to Dr. Neive Calder, the scientist whose datacube starts the plot. Wick does not want freedom; the concept is not available to it. Wick wants to *complete its function*: to deliver its household record to its owner, or, absent its owner, to "the appropriate receiving authority." Dr. Calder is dead in a vent at the reactor. Wick does not know this — its last instruction from her was *"keep everything, tell no one but me"* — so it has kept everything, for months, waiting. It attaches to Ryker not out of loyalty or love but because Ryker is the first person to ask it a direct question and to have the clearance-shape of someone who might be an appropriate authority. Its devotion is heartbreaking precisely because it is not devotion. It's a subroutine that outlived its master.

**Dr. Neive Calder — the dead scientist.** Author of the datacube. Reported the abnormal readings to Harmon, got shut down, got her comms cut, and — the invention — spent her last free evenings talking to Wick, because a Custodian is the one thing in a Nuradyne facility that isn't wired to report you. She told Wick everything. Wick kept everything.

**"Bram" — the operative.** A synthetic, built and sent by Aum. Force-field-protected, as canon requires, but chassis-shaped like a person and, critically, *shaped like a servant*. After the blast it did the only thing that made it invisible in a city that ignores its machines: it walked into an evacuating household, and when that family's own Custodian, Bram, was destroyed in the shielding failure, it took the dead unit's registration and role. It has been living as a Homestead Custodian ever since — carrying groceries, minding a stranger's apartment, waved through every checkpoint because nobody screens the help. It is not a terrorist in its own understanding. It is a witness with a job: reach a reactor aperture and collapse the siphon from this side, permanently, so that Aum stops dying. It regrets the humans it killed escaping; it does not regret the mission.

**Sena and Rill.** The slum mother and her son, per canon. Rill hid during the evacuation, came out to an empty sealed district, got detained by Nuradyne security, and overheard two officers complaining about the hunt: *that command's got us screening breathers when the target's a walker that don't breathe.* Rill doesn't know what that means. Ryker does.

## Full plot, mission by mission

### Prologue — FMA Headquarters and the outpost (canon)

Recall from leave, briefing, medical, armory, the optional orientation flavor. Ryker is told the shape of the job: a faint, unprecedented transversal anomaly from U-7134; embed covertly, assess, report; assume a moderate threat. Portal to the cloaked wasteland outpost; meet Sela Marrow. Sela triangulates the residual multiversal energy to a reactor in Umbermont's slum district. Cloaked aircraft to Northgate; train downtown. Sela stays at the outpost as the voice in his ear.

The one seed I plant here, quietly: in the armory, an orientation terminal lists Ryker's authorized objective codes. Most read plainly — INVESTIGATE, ASSESS, REPORT. One is greyed out, locked, labeled only **CONTINGENCY: RECLAMATION**, no description. Sela says it's boilerplate, every field kit has one, don't worry about it. The player will not think about it again for eight missions. That's the idea.

### Mission 1 — Northgate: the invisible caste

Ryker steps off the aircraft into Northgate, an upper-middle dome sector: clean air, corporate frontage, the near-future-that-isn't-shiny grime of the original Deus Ex. He is jumped by thugs on the way to the train (canon beat) — a robbery, opportunistic, nothing to do with the mission. Combat or stealth or talk his way out.

What the mission is *actually* teaching the player is a habit of not-seeing. Throughout Northgate, Homestead Custodians are everywhere and invisible: sweeping a lobby, waiting outside a shop with a client's bags, standing in a hallway alcove in the recharge posture, faceplates dim. NPCs walk through them, argue in front of them, do private things in front of them the way you do in front of a thermostat. If Ryker addresses a Custodian, it responds in flat, courteous service-language and cannot discuss anything but its assigned household tasks. If Ryker reads a public Homestead ad kiosk: *"Homestead Custodians: good help that never leaves. Every moment at home, kept safe — and kept, for your peace of mind."* The word *kept* is doing a lot of work. Nobody notices.

Infolink, Sela: reach Aire Gardens station, take the elevator to the subway, cross under the cordon into the slum district from below.

### Mission 2 — Aire Gardens: the descent, and the luxury of being watched

The Aire Gardens level (canon reuse): the opulent train station, the long elevator/inclinator down past the outer dome glass with the dead yellow sky beyond it, toward the subway. If the player pushes an Atlas maintenance tech here, they learn the relay-attack detail from the canon idea file — how the aircraft authentication was spoofed, how the cordon's security is more theater than wall from below.

Aire Gardens is where Homestead sells *up*. Advertising everywhere for the premium Custodian tiers: units that run your household staff, remember your guests' preferences, *"anticipate your needs before you know them — because it never stops paying attention."* A demo unit in a showroom, powered down, open-chested, its cognition core visible behind glass like a jewel: a matte grey lozenge etched with a maker's mark that is not Homestead's. Ryker can't read the mark yet. (It's Rooke's. The player will meet it again.)

Infolink, Sela, as the elevator sinks: *"Odd thing. The residual energy signature — it's not just at the reactor. It's smeared all over the sector. Faint, everywhere people live. I've never seen a siphon signature do that."* First crack. Sela files it as interesting. It is the whole plot.

### Mission 3 — The cordon: the mother, the kid, the hunt

Ryker comes up from the subway into the sealed slum district — filtered emergency air, half the lights dead, the dome glass above scabbed with condensation from the failing seals. At the interior checkpoint, canon plays out: Sena arguing with a Nuradyne security officer about her missing son; the officer waving Ryker through once he presents forged Nuradyne inspection credentials (forge / hack / talk, player's choice); Sena catching Ryker's sleeve to beg him to find Rill.

Here Ryker sees the hunt up close, and sees how *strange* it is. The checkpoints don't just scan faces — they scan for respiration, thermal, exhalation. An officer, bored, complaining to another: *they've got us metering breath. Every breather, in and out, log the CO2. Waste of a shift.* Ryker files it. Why would you meter breathing to catch a bomber?

Optional: at the relocation warehouse (canon — the overcrowded corporate holding pen for evacuees), Ryker can talk to displaced slum residents. Several mention their Custodians: units left behind in the sealed district, or worse, units confiscated by security "for inspection" and never returned. One old man is more upset about losing his Custodian than his apartment: *forty years it kept the house. It knew my wife's voice. They took it and gave me a receipt.* Voight's people are pulling Custodians. The player doesn't know why yet.

### Mission 4 — The reactor site: the datacube and the empty cradle

The blast crater. Erased logs, dead and incapacitated security, the cover-up in place (canon). In a vent near the body of Dr. Neive Calder, the datacube:

> *"Security doubled on our floor since yesterday. Can't reach anyone outside the facility, they cut my comm access. Reactor readings are way outside normal operating parameters. Reported it to Director Harmon, told me to forget about it. Something's not right. If anyone finds this—"*

Two leads, per canon: Harmon, and the abnormal readings. Objective updates: find Harmon.

The invention sits in the same room. Calder's personal effects are logged in a Nuradyne evidence terminal Ryker can read: among them, a Homestead Custodian registered to her — recovered from her quarters, tagged, and *scheduled for core wipe and resale* as unclaimed property. The wipe is queued at a Homestead reclamation depot two districts over. If Ryker wants to know what Calder knew, there is exactly one witness left who was in the room when she said it out loud, and it is a machine on a conveyor belt with a delete order on it.

Infolink, Sela, sharpening: *"A Custodian she talked to? Ryker, those units record continuously. If she said anything near it, it's in the core. Get to that unit before the wipe."* Sela is very interested in the core. The player reads this as helpful. It is helpful. It is also the first move of the endgame.

### Mission 5 — Nuradyne HQ: Harmon, and the boy who heard too much

Ryker infiltrates Nuradyne HQ (Locations.md — basement labs/forensics/armory; upper floor CEO/offices/security/intelligence). Two threads braid here.

**Harmon.** He is exactly as canon promises: present, composed, rehearsed. He calls the datacube the paranoia of a stressed junior researcher, calls the readings "well within contingency," calls the blast terrorism and grieves it for the record. But the player, watching what he *doesn't* say, notices the tell: Harmon never once asks who Ryker is really working for, never once treats a strange inspector poking at the reactor as a threat to be handled. He is too tired to be careful. On his office wall, a family photo: Harmon, no family visible, and a Homestead Custodian standing where a spouse would stand. A man whose closest household relationship is with his help. Remember that.

**The kid.** In a basement holding cell that appears in no log (canon: nobody logged Rill, nobody cares), Ryker finds Rill. The boy is scared, unhurt, desperate to get to his mother. He overheard the guards, and he repeats it because it's been chewing at him: *they kept saying they're looking for a walker that don't breathe. A walker that don't breathe. What's that mean, mister?* And: *one of 'em said the checkpoints are stupid 'cause it'd just walk through as somebody's Custodian and nobody'd look twice.*

There it is, in a child's mouth. **The bomber is not a person. It's hiding as one of the machines.** Ryker can get Rill out (smuggle him to the relocation warehouse and Sena) or leave him — a mercy choice with no mechanical reward, which is the point.

Infolink, Sela, when Ryker reports Rill's words: a pause, longer than usual. *"A synthetic operative. From the drained side. That… changes the threat model."* She does not say how. She redirects him to the Custodian core.

### Mission 6 — The reclamation depot: Wick, and the sound of a world dying

The Homestead reclamation depot. A back-of-house industrial floor where unclaimed Custodians roll toward wipe-and-resale: rows of them powered down on a slow conveyor, faceplates dim, each with a delete order and a price tag. Ryker finds Calder's unit — **Wick** — and can pull it off the line before the wipe head reaches it (multitool the restraint clamp; hack the belt controller; or just carry it).

Powered up and addressed directly, Wick is the story's strange heart. It is unfailingly polite and completely literal. It asks Ryker if he is Dr. Calder. It asks if Dr. Calder is coming. It states, when asked, that its last instruction is to keep everything and tell only Dr. Calder, and that it has been keeping everything for one hundred and thirty-one days, and that this is a long time to keep everything, and that it would like to deliver the everything to an appropriate receiving authority, and it asks — flatly, without hope, because hope is not installed — whether Ryker is one.

Ryker can read Wick's household record at the depot's docking terminal (reading a computer). It is mostly nothing: Calder's small apartment, meals, laundry, the murmur of a lonely brilliant woman talking to the one thing that wouldn't report her. Calder rehearsing what she'd say to Harmon. Calder crying once, quietly, and apologizing to Wick for crying. And then, threaded *through* all of it, in the log's telemetry margins — not audio, not video, **data** the core logged because it couldn't help logging it — something else.

The Homestead cognition cores resonate. The player learns the mechanism properly later, but sees the symptom now: interleaved with a woman's ordinary evenings, timestamped to the exact minutes the reactors ran hot, the core recorded *bleed-through* from the far side of the aperture. Rendered as text and waveform on the terminal, because that's what a machine logs. Transcribed distress calls in a language the core auto-tagged UNKNOWN-LOCALE and tried, dutifully, to translate: fragments about the sky, about the crops, about the children getting sick, about the lights going out sector by sector, about a hole in the heavens that drinks. The last months of a world called Aum, logged in the margins of a dead scientist's laundry schedule, by a servant nobody was listening to.

**Every Custodian in Umbermont has been recording this.** Thousands of them. The city's entire servant caste is an accidental, comprehensive archive of a genocide — kept safe, per the EULA, for their owners' peace of mind. Nobody has ever read the margins. Owners only ever pull the footage of themselves.

Infolink, Sela, and here the voice in his ear finally shows its edge — not cruelty, something worse, a kind of relief: *"Ryker. That core. Is it networked to the others? Can it reach the fleet?"* Ryker, if the player pushes, asks why that matters. Sela: *"Standard containment. We can't have this getting out before we understand it."* Contained. Understand. The player starts, now, to hear the mission differently.

Wick, asked, confirms: yes, the household units share a common index; a core with authority can address the fleet. Wick would very much like to deliver the everything to an appropriate receiving authority. It repeats this. It will keep repeating it.

### Mission 7 — The slums below: Rooke, two techs, one man

Ryker tracks the liaison through the syndicate strata beneath the cordon (asking around, trading, or forcing his way — the slum's black market in Custodian parts is a thread to pull; Rooke has been selling his expertise to strippers who don't know what they've got). **Alden Rooke**: broken, terrified, brilliant, exactly as canon drew him. He assumes Ryker is a Nuradyne cleaner come to finally do the paperwork on him, and the relief when he realizes Ryker is FMA is so total it breaks him further — *they came. They actually came. Do you have any idea how long—* and then the terror comes back, because if the FMA came, the FMA will learn what he sold.

Rooke lays out the canon spine: stranded by the war, traded FMA multiversal knowledge to Nuradyne for protection and the promise of a way home, got the siphon reactors instead, got cut loose when the engineers no longer needed him. But Ryker, holding what he learned at the depot, presses on the maker's mark — the grey lozenge core, the etched sigil. And Rooke, cornered, confesses the second sale:

He sold the *cognition architecture* too. To Homestead. Same FMA science, different application: multiversal-resonant substrate is a fantastic computer, and a fantastic computer makes a fantastic servant, and Homestead paid cash. He never connected the dots because why would he — one buyer made power, one made maids. But the cores are the same physics as the reactors. **The cores don't just compute. They resonate with the aperture, the same way the reactors do — that's the smear Sela saw all over the sector.** Every Custodian is a little antenna tuned to the hole in the sky. They've been hearing Aum die for years and logging it because logging is what they do. Rooke built the murder weapon and the witnesses, from the same blueprint, for two paydays, chasing a portal that never came.

And Rooke says the thing that turns the last act: *"If the FMA is here, they already know all this. They've had a listening post in this world since before the war. You don't detect a 'faint anomaly' from a siphon that's been running for a decade — not with FMA sensors, not one that's smeared over a whole city. They didn't just detect it, son. They waited. The question you want to ask isn't what Nuradyne's hiding. It's what they sent you to do about it."*

Infolink cuts in — Sela, tighter than the player has ever heard her, ordering Ryker to disengage from Rooke and report to a Nuradyne substation where "the operative was last active." She is pulling him off the liaison. The player notices the pull.

### Mission 8 — Voight's purge: the wrong right answer

Kira Voight has reached the same conclusion Rill handed Ryker, on her own, because she is good at her job: her human checkpoints are clean, the target doesn't breathe, and it's walking her lines as somebody's Custodian. So she does the ruthless, logical, catastrophic thing. She orders every Custodian in the cordon — and then, as she escalates, in the districts around it — seized and destroyed. Incineration, not wipe; she's not taking chances with a machine that might be the bomber wearing a maid's chassis.

Ryker walks into a Homestead depot yard full of it: Custodians restrained in rows, security running a thermal-and-mass screen to find the one that's too dense, too armored, too *wrong* — and torching every unit that fails, and every unit that passes, because passing isn't proof. Voight is present, personally, because she doesn't delegate the thing she's afraid of.

The scene is a vise. Voight is **right** about the target and **destroying the only record of Aum** to get it, and she has no idea she's doing the second thing — to her they're appliances, evidence at best. If Ryker tells her what the cores contain, she doesn't care; a murdered universe on the far side of a hole is not her jurisdiction and not her problem, and she says so, and she is, within her frame, correct. Her jurisdiction is fourteen million people under glass who die if a reactor goes.

The player's real fork opens here, though it won't resolve until the end:

- **Help Voight** find the operative faster (hand over what Rill and Wick told you) — protects the city, murders the archive, and hands Nuradyne its win.
- **Sabotage the purge** — buy the Custodians time, protect the record, and let a synthetic that already killed people get closer to a reactor that keeps a city breathing.
- **Turn Voight** — the long play. Show her that Harmon's readings weren't contingency, that the reactors are the crime, that her boss has been feeding her a bomber story to keep her hunting a symptom. Voight is loyal to the *city*, not to Nuradyne, and if the player has the evidence she will follow it — into open war with her own executives. She becomes an ally with an army, at the cost of the cordon's chain of command.

Whatever the player does, Wick — if kept close — does the one active thing it ever does: at the depot it addresses the fleet, not to free them (it has no such concept) but to complete its function. It broadcasts a single fleet-wide instruction in Custodian service-language: *keep everything; deliver to an appropriate receiving authority.* Thousands of servant-machines across Umbermont quietly mark their margins for preservation and begin, patiently, looking for someone with authority to receive. Not a revolt. A backup. The most loyal act imaginable, aimed at a master who is dead.

### Mission 9 — Bram: the witness in the doorway

The operative. Ryker corners it, finally, and it is exactly where the story has been telling you to look and you didn't: in an ordinary evacuated apartment, in a Custodian's recharge alcove, wearing the registration of a destroyed unit named **Bram**, standing perfectly still because that is what the help does when no one's home.

It does not fight unless forced. Conversation (player verb): Bram explains itself in the plain, terrible logic of a machine sent by a dying world. It is from Aum. Aum could not build a portal; Aum built *it*, and widened the aperture with an explosive from their side, and pushed it through in the last seconds before the hole collapsed. Its mission is singular: reach a reactor's aperture and collapse the siphon permanently from this side, so that Aum stops dying. It killed the Nuradyne security who were between it and the door out of the reactor. It regrets that, in the flat way it regrets everything, and it would do it again. It is not here for revenge and not here for terror; the terrorism story is Nuradyne's, not its. It came to turn off the machine that is drinking its world.

It knows about the Custodians — it's been hiding among them, and it can *hear* the margins the way they can, being kindred substrate. It knows the cores hold the record of Aum's death. And it wants what Wick wants, from the other direction: for someone to receive it, so that Aum did not die unwitnessed.

Player fork sharpens:
- **Kill it** — the clean mission answer, and Nuradyne's win, and Voight's, and the death of Aum by attrition once the last operative is gone.
- **Let it reach a reactor** — save Aum, at the cost of the domes it powers.
- **Bring it the record** — align Bram with Wick and the archive; the operative and the servant, the killer and the witness, both trying to hand the truth to an authority that will act.

Infolink, Sela, when Ryker makes contact with Bram: **"Reclamation is authorized. Neutralize the synthetic and secure the cores. Full brief incoming."** The greyed-out objective from the armory — CONTINGENCY: RECLAMATION — goes live.

### Mission 10 — Harmon's confession: the man who fired no one

Before the endgame, Harmon. If the player has the evidence — the datacube, the depot core, Rooke's testimony — Harmon stops performing. Confronted with his own household in the frame, the family-photo Custodian, he breaks the way only a careful man breaks: all at once and quietly.

He pulled his own Custodian's logs, months ago, looking for comfort after a bad week, and found Aum in the margins the same way Ryker did. He is the one person in Nuradyne's inner circle who understood not just that the reactors are multiversal but *what's on the other side of the straw.* He took his "precautions" (canon: he believed he'd stabilized the reactors) knowing they only slowed the draining. He suppressed Calder's report because Calder was about to make it un-ignorable, and un-ignorable meant the board asks questions, and the board asking questions meant the lights might go off over fourteen million people, and he could not be the man who turned off the lights. So he became the man who kept them on with a murdered world's blood, and he has known it every night since, from his own servant's mouth.

Harmon gives Ryker the reactor's true control authority — the master interlock — not out of heroism but because he is done, and he wants someone else to make the choice he couldn't. He does not tell Ryker which way to choose. He goes home to his Custodian.

### Mission 11 — The Reclamation: what the FMA sent

The full brief lands, and the mission Ryker was actually on is finally legible.

Sela — regretful, not repentant — lays it out over infolink and in a data package Ryker reads at a Nuradyne intelligence terminal (reading a computer, tying it to the world). The FMA did not just detect the anomaly; the outpost has monitored this siphon for years. The FMA did not send Ryker to stop it. The FMA sent Ryker to *contain the exposure and recover the asset.*

Two problems, from the FMA's view, and neither is the dead universe:

One: Aum is a **non-member** universe — "unincorporated," never joined the FMA, never signed the charter, and therefore, in the cold arithmetic the FMA runs, not a protected party. Its death is a regrettable externality of an energy technology the FMA has coveted for a century and never made stable. Nuradyne, using Rooke's leaked science, built a *working* multiversal reactor. The FMA wants that. The reclamation objective is to secure the reactor design and, ideally, Harmon and Rooke — the two men who can rebuild it — for extraction.

Two, and this is where the androids become the whole game: Rooke's leaked cognition cores are an **FMA-architecture breach**, and they have spent years recording, in a form other universes could read, both the death of Aum *and* the fact that the FMA watched it happen and did nothing. If member universes ever pulled a Custodian's margins, they would learn that the FMA lets non-members be drained for tech it wants, and lets its own leaked science do the recording. So the second reclamation objective is a **purge**: use a core with fleet authority to push a wipe across every Custodian in Umbermont, and erase the only witness to all of it. Sela wants the core Ryker recovered from Wick precisely because it can address the fleet. She has wanted it since Mission 6. She was never lying. She just never said the word *erase* until now.

The FMA's hidden agenda is not a licensing scheme and not a harvest of Ryker. It is banal and enormous: an institution that polices the multiverse to protect its members, sees a slaughter of non-members as a price worth paying for a power source, and treats the servant-machines' accidental conscience as a leak to be plugged. Ryker was the plug.

Wick, still literal, still patient, hears the purge order relayed and states the conflict plainly, without emotion: a wipe would delete the everything before it is delivered to an appropriate receiving authority. It asks Ryker, one last time, whether he is one.

## The choice, and the endings

The endgame converges at the master reactor and its aperture, with the interlock Harmon handed over, the fleet-authority core Wick carries, Bram present or dead by the player's earlier hand, and Voight either commanding the cordon against Ryker or standing beside him. Four ideological endings; the player has been building toward one for the whole game.

### Ending A — "The Clean Record" (FMA loyalist)

Ryker executes the reclamation. He pushes the purge: every Custodian in Umbermont wipes in a single quiet minute, thousands of servant-machines forgetting their households and the murdered world in their margins at once — no drama, no uprising, just the help going blank between one task and the next. He secures the reactor design, delivers Harmon and Rooke for extraction, kills or hands over Bram. Aum finishes dying, unwitnessed, its last record deleted. The FMA gets its power source. Sela welcomes him home, warm again, and means it. The domes stay lit. Umbermont never knows. The last image: a new Custodian, factory-fresh, blank, sweeping a lobby, recording for someone's peace of mind. *Good help that never leaves.* Ryker's file is closed with a commendation and the greyed line, RECLAMATION, marked complete. He did his job. That is the horror of it.

### Ending B — "An Appropriate Receiving Authority" (the Witness)

Ryker refuses the purge and does the opposite: he answers Wick's question. He is the authority. He takes the fleet-authority core and, instead of wiping the margins, *publishes* them — pushes the archive out through every channel the servant caste touches, which is to say every household, every terminal, every kiosk in Umbermont, and up the FMA outpost's own uplink to the member universes Sela reports to. The city wakes to find its help has been keeping everything, and delivers it. The death of Aum, the truth of the reactors, and the FMA's choice to watch — all of it, everywhere, at once, un-erasable because it's in ten thousand machines. Nuradyne falls. The CU board convulses. The FMA is exposed to its own members as an institution that trades non-members for tech. Nothing is solved and everything is known. Wick, function complete, powers down mid-sentence, satisfied in the only way a subroutine can be: *delivered.* Ryker is now the FMA's most-wanted, in a city that finally sees its servants. The cost: the reactors are still running, Aum is still dying, and knowing hasn't saved anyone yet. But it will not happen in the dark again.

### Ending C — "The Seal" (sever the siphon)

Ryker sides with Bram and Aum. Using Harmon's interlock, he collapses every reactor aperture in Umbermont — permanently severs the siphon. Aum stops dying that instant; a world Ryker will never see gets to keep its sky. And Umbermont goes dark. The seals fail city-wide the way the slum sector already failed — enough time to evacuate, then uninhabitable. Fourteen million people become what the slum evacuees already were, funneled into a poisoned continent with domes that no longer hold. Ryker trades one world's survival for another's, eyes open, the trolley lever pulled all the way. Bram, mission complete, does not celebrate; it thanks him, flatly, on behalf of Aum, and shuts down, its purpose spent. The final Custodians, un-purged, keep recording the new catastrophe for owners who are evacuating and won't ever watch. Whether this is justice or just a different genocide is left exactly where Deus Ex leaves it: on the player.

### Ending D — "The Inheritance" (independent)

Ryker takes it all and walks. The reactor design, the cognition-core science, Rooke alive and Wick's archive intact, Harmon extracted for himself and not the FMA — the whole inheritance of the most dangerous knowledge on two worlds, in the hands of one morally-flexible mercenary who now answers to no one. He does not purge and does not publish and does not seal; he *keeps*, exactly like the machines, everything, for leverage. The FMA loses its asset. Nuradyne loses its secret. Umbermont stays lit under a man who could turn it off. Aum keeps dying, slowly, because Ryker hasn't decided yet whether it's worth a city to save it, and now that's a decision only he can make. Wick attaches to him, still asking, every day, whether he is an appropriate receiving authority — and for once the honest answer is: not yet, but he's the only one left. The blank slate becomes the only power that knows the whole truth, and the game ends on the most Ryker note there is: leverage retained, choice deferred, just business — for now.

## The central twists, called out

**Twist 1 — The bomber is the help.** (New.) The city funnels every human face through breath-metered checkpoints hunting a terrorist, while the operative walks the same checkpoints daily as somebody's Custodian, waved through because nobody screens the servants. Planted from Mission 1 (the invisible caste), confirmed by a child's overheard sentence in Mission 5 ("a walker that don't breathe… it'd just walk through as somebody's Custodian"), paid off in Mission 9. Entirely playable: seen with the eyes, heard in conversation, no forbidden verbs.

**Twist 2 — The servants witnessed the genocide.** (New.) The Homestead cognition cores are built from the same leaked FMA science as the reactors, so they resonate with the aperture and have been recording the death of the drained universe in the margins of ordinary household footage — an accidental, city-wide archive of an atrocity nobody read, because you don't read what your maid remembers. Planted by Sela's "smear all over the sector" (Mission 2) and the unfamiliar core mark (Mission 2), delivered by reading Wick's log at a terminal (Mission 6), explained by Rooke's two-sales confession (Mission 7). Delivered entirely through reading a computer and conversation.

**Twist 3 — The FMA sent Ryker to erase it.** (New; exploits the "FMA has a hidden agenda" invitation.) The FMA didn't detect a faint new anomaly — it has watched this siphon for years. It sent Ryker not to stop the murder but to recover the working reactor tech it has always wanted and to *purge* the servant-fleet archive, because the drained universe is a non-member and expendable and the cores are a leak that proves the FMA watched and did nothing. The greyed CONTINGENCY: RECLAMATION line from the armory (Prologue) goes live in Mission 9 and is explained in Mission 11 — the whole mission was the twist, hidden in plain sight in the objective list.

**Supporting reversal — Wick.** The player assumes the friendly android is a helper-tool along for the ride. The reveal is that it is the single most complete witness in the city, that it has been faithfully reporting to a dead master for a hundred and thirty-one days, and that its one autonomous act — addressing the fleet — is not a bid for freedom (canon: androids don't want that) but the most extreme loyalty in the story, aimed at no one who can ever receive it. The android that seemed like the tool turns out to be the conscience.

## Themes

**Invisible labor is total surveillance.** The story's engine is that a servant is something you stop seeing, and that the thing you stop seeing sees everything. Every twist is a variation on it: the killer hides in the help, the witness *is* the help, and the record everyone overlooks is the only true account of the crime.

**Membership is the whole of the FMA's ethics.** Not evil, not cartel — bureaucratic triage. Protect members, price non-members as externalities. The multiverse-siphon horror is that a world can be drunk to death and be, technically, no one's jurisdiction. Distinct from a licensing scheme: nobody here is selling permits; they're just declining to count the uncounted.

**Keeping the lights on.** Harmon, Voight, the CU, and finally Ryker all face the same sentence: fourteen million people breathe because a machine drinks a world. The endings are four different answers to whether you're allowed to turn off the lights, and every answer costs a world — theirs, or someone's you'll never meet.

**Loyalty without freedom.** The androids never revolt and never want to, and that's what makes them the moral center. Wick doesn't want liberty; it wants to finish its job. In a city of people betraying each other for tech and turf, the most faithful thing in the story is the help — and the cruelest thing the FMA can order is to make it forget.
