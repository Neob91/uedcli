# The Caretaker Clause

Run 2 | Writer: opus | Emphasis: Ryker goes rogue / hunted by the FMA

**Logline.** An FMA investigator sent into a poisoned dome-city to trace an illegal energy siphon discovers that his agency never meant to bring him home — that "investigator" was a cover for a one-way posting the FMA reserves for the universes it has already decided to abandon. Cut off from the portal and hunted by the corporation he came to expose, Liam Ryker has to build a life and an army out of the exact people his own agency wrote off: a broken predecessor, a foreign saboteur, a mother, and a city that doesn't know it's already been condemned.

---

## What is fixed vs. what I invented

**Fixed (canon, kept intact):** Ryker the blank-slate ex-military merc; the FMA policing the multiverse; U-7134 / Quinmore; the Corporate Union of dome-cities in a chemically poisoned world; Nuradyne (power) siphoning energy from parallel universes; Atlas Subsystems (shielding); the drained universe that traced the siphon and sent a force-field operative who blew the slum reactor and fled; the stranded FMA liaison who sold Nuradyne the multiversal knowledge and was then discarded; Director Harmon (suppressed the abnormal readings); Kira Voight (Chief of Security); the outpost partner feeding objectives; the missing slum kid who overheard something; the whole prologue → outpost → Northgate → cordon → datacube → Harmon → holding-cell chain; the player-verb limits.

**Invented:** the **Caretaker Clause** — FMA doctrine that compromised universes are sealed, not policed, and staffed by a single deliberately-stranded resident agent (this is the new central twist, and it recasts the canon liaison's "stranded by the war" as the *cover story* for a decommissioned caretaker); the partner **Sable** and the **Coordinator**; the operative's name **Sela** and the reveal that her universe is a **previously-sealed FMA cast-off**; Atlas engineer **Rhona Tam**; the slum fixer **Bex** and the Ashline crew; the mother **Neve** and son **Toby**; the undercity secondary aperture; four endings.

---

## Voice & how it reads

Cold and procedural on the surface, the way Ryker is. The story is told the way he'd file it: clipped, present-focused, everything a fact until it isn't. The horror arrives as paperwork. The emotional weight is carried by mirrors — every person Ryker meets is a version of what the FMA does to people, and the back half is Ryker refusing to become the last one.

# ACT ONE — The Investigation They Wrote For Him

## Mission 1 — Recall (FMA Headquarters)

The prologue plays as canon. Ryker is pulled off leave. A low-level clerk routes him to briefing. The **Coordinator** — a career handler, unhurried, the kind of person who has sent a lot of people to a lot of places — lays out the anomaly: a faint, unprecedented transversal signal from U-7134, a universe the FMA has had no live contact with since before its war. Not confirmed travel. Not confirmed anything. Go quietly, find out, report.

Medical clears him. The armory kits him for underground work and a lethal atmosphere. Optional orientation beats let the player wander HQ and overhear the shape of the FMA: vast, bureaucratic, older than any single Earth, the multiverse's traffic cops.

**Planted here (twist seeds):**
- The Coordinator reads from Ryker's file, half to himself: *"No dependents, no standing attachments, cleared for indefinite deployment. Good. We build the long postings around men like you."* Ryker, being Ryker, doesn't react. The player might.
- A junior analyst, if the player talks to her during orientation, mentions she processes "resident postings" and asks — innocently — whether Ryker's had his *residency medical* yet, then catches herself: "Sorry. Wrong file, probably."
- The mission designation on the armory manifest, readable at a terminal, is not **INVESTIGATE/EXFIL**. It's a code the player can't parse yet: **CTKR-7134**. It scrolls past. It will matter.

Ryker portals to the outpost with his partner, call sign **Sable**.

## Mission 2 — The Listening Post (Wasteland Outpost → Northgate)

The cloaked FMA bunker sits in dead territory nobody has a reason to cross. Sable stays here to run "independent research" and feed objectives by infolink — canon. The outpost's sensors pick up residual multiversal energy and Sable triangulates it to a reactor in Quinmore's slum district. Cloaked aircraft to **Northgate**; then the downtown train.

Northgate is the player's first breath of the world: sealed sky, filtered light the color of weak tea, corporate signage bolted over pre-war stonework — a real old town with a logo stamped on it. Thugs jump Ryker near the station (canon beat). He handles it however the player likes. A fixer watching the fight — **Bex**, of the slum crew called the **Ashline** — clocks that Ryker fights like someone trained by an institution, not a street, and files it away. Bex becomes a contact later.

**Planted here:**
- Sable's infolink chatter runs warm and personal in a way that reads, on a second play, as wrong for a short op: *"Get the lay of it. Learn the streets. You're going to want to know this city."* And later: *"How are the locals treating you? You'll want them warming to you."* Investigators don't need locals to warm to them.
- On the train, a slum drunk mutters about **the Ferryman** — a madman in the undercity who "talks to the sky-people" and "knew the reactor would burn before it burned." Everyone laughs him off. That's the liaison. That's the seed.

## Mission 3 — The Cordon (Slum District Entrance)

Canon, played straight. Nuradyne has cordoned the blast zone under corporate-jurisdiction law; checkpoints screen every evacuee. Publicly: hunting the bomber. Actually: hunting the operative. Ryker gets in by forged credentials / hacking / social engineering — the player's approach.

At the entrance, a mother — **Neve** — is arguing with a Nuradyne officer about her missing son, **Toby**. The officer waves her off and turns on Ryker; Ryker asserts clearance; the officer lets him through. Neve catches his sleeve and asks him to find her boy. Ryker, low-affect, doesn't promise. The objective logs itself anyway.

**Planted here:**
- The checkpoint isn't screening for a *bomb-maker's profile*. Ryker can read a security terminal (player verb): the match criteria are **thermal-anomalous** and **field-signature positive** — they're scanning for something that *glows*, something wrapped in a force field. Not a terrorist. A person made of light. Ryker doesn't have the frame for it yet. The player banks it.

## Mission 4 — The Reactor Site

Canon. Logs erased, security dead or down. Ryker works the site. Near a dead scientist, in a vent: the datacube.

> *"Security doubled on our floor since yesterday. Can't reach anyone outside the facility, they cut my comm access. Reactor readings are way outside normal operating parameters. Reported it to Director Harmon, told me to forget about it. Something's not right. If anyone finds this—"*

Two leads: **Harmon**, and the **abnormal readings**. Next objective: Harmon.

**Planted here:**
- The blast pattern is wrong for a bomb. The reactor didn't just explode — something came *out* of it. A ring of scorched floor radiates from the reactor's central aperture *outward*, and the dead security are all facing *inward*, cut down while advancing on the core, not fleeing a device. Ryker can note this at a workstation. The reactor is a door, and something walked through it. (Reader knows: the operative. Ryker will meet her.)
- One surviving reactor log fragment, half-corrupted, isn't a maintenance note. It's a warning to Harmon from someone signing as **"external consult, deprecated."** The Ferryman. The liaison consulted on this reactor and was ignored.

## Mission 5 — Harmon Stonewalls (Nuradyne HQ)

Canon. Harmon is at his desk, business as usual — vanishing would be suspicious. He stonewalls, recites the terrorism line, dismisses the datacube as a nervous man's ghost story. What he says and what he steers around are different things. He never once asks *who Ryker really is* — which is itself strange; a real director facing an unknown investigator would dig. Harmon acts like he already half-expected someone to come, and half-expected them to be worse.

**Planted here:**
- If the player reads Harmon's office computer (player verb), one archived message stands out — from Voight to Harmon: *"The Authority's asset is a problem we may not have to solve. Their book on this place is nearly closed. Sit tight."* **"The Authority."** **"Their book."** Nuradyne *knows about the FMA.* And they know the FMA is about to "close the book." The player doesn't know what that means. It reads like Nuradyne being reassured that Ryker's own people are about to make him irrelevant. Reading it triggers a Sable infolink: *"Harmon's dirty, obviously. Stay on the readings. And Ryker — don't force anything at HQ yet. We only get one clean window here. Don't waste it."* One clean window. He says it a lot.

## Mission 6 — The Kid in the Cell

Canon. Mid-investigation, in a Nuradyne holding facility, Ryker finds **Toby** — grabbed for being in the sealed district, unlogged, forgotten. While detained he overheard security talk. He doesn't understand it. Ryker does — or starts to.

Toby overheard two things:
1. *"The old man in the undercity called it. Said the sky-people would come to close the book on this whole city."* (The Ferryman predicted the FMA would seal U-7134.)
2. *"They're not looking for a bomber. They're looking for the thing that came out of the reactor. And Voight says if the Authority's asset finds it first, let him. Cleaner that way."*

So: Nuradyne knows there's an "Authority asset" (Ryker) loose in the city, and their play is to *let him* find the operative and then clean up both. Ryker is being used as a bird dog by the very corporation he's investigating. And there's a man underground who saw all of it coming.

Ryker gets Toby out or arranges it. Neve's thread pays a small warmth here — the only warmth Ryker's had. Objective: **the abnormal readings**, which trace down, into the undercity. To the Ferryman.

# ACT TWO — The Book on Quinmore

## Mission 7 — Aire Gardens, and the Long Way Down

The readings lead through **Aire Gardens** — a luxury transit hub, the train station reused, a real inclinator that drops the whole way to the subway (per the design notes). On the ride down the player watches Quinmore stratify: the bright dome-tops give way to service levels, then the wet dark of the undercity where the poor live under everyone else's floor. Advertising for lives no one down here will ever have slides past the glass.

If the player pushes an Aire Gardens tech, they learn how the operative's arrival aircraft-authentication was spoofed weeks ago (relay attack) — establishing that someone with real technical reach has been operating in Quinmore off the books. Not the FMA. Not exactly Nuradyne. A third hand.

In the subway dark, Ryker finds a **secondary aperture** — a smaller, cruder siphon node, older than the slum reactor, half-cannibalized for parts. This is where the Ferryman lives. This is where he first taught Nuradyne how the trick was done.

## Mission 8 — The Ferryman (First Mirror)

**Elias Marsh**, called the **Ferryman**, is the stranded FMA liaison of canon: the man who traded multiversal knowledge to Nuradyne for protection and a life in the domes, then got discarded when their engineers no longer needed him. Bitter, terrified, tolerated only because he's not worth the paperwork.

He knows what Ryker is the instant he sees the kit.

> *"They sent another one. Standard load-out, standard face, standard dead eyes. I had those eyes. Sit down, son. You don't know yet, and I'm going to be the one who tells you, because nobody told me and look what I did."*

Marsh delivers the **siphon truth**, the core canon secret: Nuradyne's reactors don't *generate* — they *steal*. Every reactor is a straw punched into a neighboring universe, draining it to keep Quinmore's domes lit. He tells Ryker what it did to the universes on the other end because he's the one who handed Nuradyne the math. He's spent years listening on jury-rigged FMA gear to worlds going quiet one after another, universes he helped kill, unable to stop it, unable to go home, unable to die because dying would mean it was over and he can't let himself off that easy.

And he tells Ryker the thing that reframes everything — the seed of the new twist, delivered as a broken old man's obsession:

> *"You think the war stranded me. That's what they let me think for years. But I've had a long time down here with their own equipment, and I read the traffic, and I'll tell you what I know now. The FMA doesn't leave men behind by accident, Ryker. It leaves them behind on purpose. There's a word for us in their files. Caretaker. You look for a universe that's gone wrong — gone dangerous, gone off the leash — and you don't waste a fleet policing it. You seal it. And you leave one man inside to keep an eye on the box. One expendable man, no family, no way out, so he can't defect and can't come home and spends the rest of his life managing the mess because it's the only thing keeping him breathing. That was me. That's the job. And the poor bastard sitting in front of me right now — that's you. They didn't send you to investigate. They sent you to stay."*

Ryker doesn't believe it. Not yet. Marsh is a madman; everyone says so. But the word — **Caretaker** — matches the code from the armory manifest. **CTKR-7134.** The player, at least, feels the floor tilt.

Marsh gives Ryker two things: a route to the operative (he's tracked her — she's been coming to *him*, drawn to the only other person in the city who talks about other worlds), and a warning: *"When you tell your handler what you've found, watch what they do. Watch whether they want you home. That's the tell. That's when I knew — the day the extraction never came and the reasons kept coming."*

## Mission 9 — The Operative (Sela) and the Second Book

Ryker tracks the force-field operative — **Sela** — through the flooded slum district (the sealed sector, toxic air, Ryker in his rig, her in her field). She's not a mad bomber. She came through the reactor aperture to kill the siphon at its source and buy her dying world one more generation. She's cornered, hunted by Nuradyne, starving — the field that keeps her alive is also a cage; she can't take it off in Quinmore's air and she can't sustain it forever.

First contact can go lethal (canon allows kill/incap) — but the *intended* path is the player realizing they share an enemy. Ryker can talk. What Sela describes about her home lands the **second twist**:

> *"We had the sky-people once. Your Authority. Traders, watchers, a door in the mountains. Then one year the door went dark and never opened again. No one came. No one answered. We didn't know why. We got old and poor and forgotten, and then — decades later — something started draining us from the inside, from a world we couldn't see, and still no one came. So I came to them."*

A universe the FMA **sealed** decades ago. Cut off. Left with a caretaker and a closed door — and then, generations later, drained to death by *another* universe's siphon while the FMA that sealed them looked away. Sela's people are what Quinmore is scheduled to become. **The FMA's own sealing doctrine created the desperate, tech-scavenging, retaliating world that blew Nuradyne's reactor in the first place.** The inciting incident of the entire game is the FMA's abandonment policy coming back around. Sealing doesn't contain the danger. Sealing *manufactures* it.

Ryker now has the shape of it, even if he won't say it: the FMA doesn't stop this. The FMA *farms* it — abandons a world, leaves a caretaker, and moves on before the wreckage becomes its problem.

Alliance offered. Sela can reopen and enlarge an aperture from the mechanical side — it's what she does. That capability is the fulcrum of the whole back half. But every use of it drains *something.*

## Mission 10 — The Window Closes (Midpoint Betrayal)

Ryker reports up. This is the hinge. He tells Sable the truth: the reactors are a multiversal siphon; Nuradyne knows about the FMA; there's a stranded FMA man down here who's been feeding them for years; and there's an operative from a sealed universe loose in the city. By every rule Ryker knows, this is a five-alarm escalation. This is when the FMA sends a team and burns the whole thing down.

Sable goes quiet. Too long. Then the orders come back — and they aren't Sable's, they're the **Coordinator's**, relayed flat:

> *"Understood and expected. New standing orders, effective now. Do not exfiltrate. Do not engage Nuradyne further. Maintain position. You are hereby the resident officer for substrate U-7134. The aperture at the outpost is being collapsed to prevent leakage in either direction. Congratulations on a clean embed, Ryker. The book on 7134 is closed. Sable out."*

The portal home is gone. The outpost is going dark with Ryker on the wrong side of it. Everything Marsh said was true. Ryker wasn't sent to fix Quinmore. He was sent to *be left in it* — the new caretaker for a universe the FMA decided, before he ever stepped through, to abandon. **CTKR-7134** was never a mission code. It was a job title. His.

To deliver this within the player verbs, the reveal is completed at a terminal, not a cutscene. Ryker fights back to the FMA outpost (or reaches a field relay) and reads the mission file directly (player verb: read computers):

- His posting: **CARETAKER, SUBSTRATE U-7134 — INDEFINITE. Repatriation: N/A.** Dated *before* the prologue.
- The doctrine memo: *"Compromised substrates are not repatriated or policed. A resident caretaker maintains passive observation. Aperture sealed post-embed. Caretaker selection: unattached, adaptable, self-sustaining."* (Every word the Coordinator used about Ryker in Mission 1.)
- The caretaker roster for U-7134: **Marsh, E. — decommissioned.** **Ryker, L. — active.** Two names. Same box. The Ferryman is not a cautionary tale. He's Ryker's job description with a career of seniority.
- And, on the "sealed substrates" ledger: Sela's universe, sealed 41 years ago. Marsh's stranding, dated to "the war." Quinmore, newly added, **book closed today.** The pattern is a machine and Ryker is a part inside it.

Reading it triggers Marsh's infolink: *"You found it. Now you know what I am. Now you know what you are. The only question left is whether you're going to spend the next thirty years being it."*

Ryker is now stranded. And hunted from two directions at once.

## Mission 11 — Two Hunts

**Nuradyne** wants him dead now for the ordinary reason: he knows the siphon secret, he's touched the operative, and with the FMA "closing the book," Voight's calculus flips — the Authority asset stopped being a useful bird dog the moment his own people cut him loose. Kill him, bury him with the operative and the old man, and the last witnesses to what the reactors really are die in a sealed district no one's allowed into. Clean.

**The FMA** doesn't want him dead. It wants him *quiet and resident.* As long as Ryker keeps his head down and plays caretaker, the FMA is content — that's the whole point of the doctrine. But if he tries to force the sealed aperture, or broadcast the truth into the FMA network, he stops being a caretaker and becomes exactly what Sela's world became before it was abandoned: **a rogue node** — and the FMA has a word for those too, and a team that handles them. Sable, still on the far side of a collapsing aperture, becomes the ambivalent voice of this: not an enemy exactly, a man who followed orders and now has to watch what his orders did, feeding Ryker the occasional warning against his own agency's interest, unable to open the door but unwilling to fully close his mouth. The partner as the story's conscience-in-a-box — another kind of stranded man.

Stranded, condemned, mirrored on every side, Ryker does the one thing the doctrine never plans for: instead of managing the box quietly, he starts recruiting inside it.

# ACT THREE — The Army of the Written-Off

The back half's engine: **the FMA abandons people; Ryker collects them.** Every ally is someone the system discarded, and the plot is Ryker converting the doctrine's waste products into a force. He has no portal, no agency backing, no cover. He has a city full of the condemned, which turns out to be more than the FMA ever accounted for.

## Mission 12 — Keep the Ferryman Breathing

The instant the FMA closes the book, Voight moves to tie off loose ends — starting with the undercity ghost who knows too much. Nuradyne security descends on the secondary aperture to erase Marsh. Ryker has to get there first.

This mission is where Ryker chooses what Marsh is *for.* The old man is ready to die and wants to hand Ryker his life's work — years of intercepted FMA traffic, the siphon math, the aperture mechanics — and then be done. The player can:
- **Extract him** — keep Marsh alive as an asset and a conscience, which costs and complicates everything after but keeps the story's mirror walking and talking.
- **Take the data and let him go** — Marsh dies on his own terms, and Ryker inherits the box completely, alone. (Foreshadows the Caretaker ending.)
- Fail to protect him and lose the data — hard-mode branch; Ryker rebuilds the siphon knowledge from Harmon instead.

Either way, Ryker walks out with the two things that make the endings possible: **how the siphon works** and **how an aperture can be opened.**

## Mission 13 — The Wedge (Atlas)

Ryker needs resources no slum can give him. Enter **Rhona Tam**, an Atlas Subsystems field engineer. Atlas builds the dome shielding; the slum-reactor blast killed Atlas's kit and its backups, and Atlas publicly blames Nuradyne's lax reactor security while privately suspecting the whole incident is a Nuradyne power play. Tam has been quietly trying to prove it and getting stonewalled by her own board, which doesn't want a war with the corp that controls the power grid.

Ryker gives her the one thing that turns suspicion into a weapon: **the reactors are a siphon.** Not cutting-edge power tech — theft from other worlds, hidden from the CU board that approved them. That's not a safety scandal; that's fraud against the entire Corporate Union, and it makes every dome in Quinmore dependent on an energy source that can trigger retaliation from universes no one on the board knows exist.

Tam becomes Ryker's corporate wedge: safehouses, credentials, movement through the upper city, and — critically — a channel to the **CU board**, the one authority that outranks Nuradyne. She has her own agenda (Atlas on top, Nuradyne broken) and her own line she won't cross (she will not doom the domes to hurt Nuradyne). Ambiguous ally. Useful exactly as far as their interests run parallel.

## Mission 14 — The Ashline (The Street)

Bex and the **Ashline** crew — smugglers who move contraband and people through the undercity and the sealed sector Nuradyne thinks it controls — are the muscle and the map. They don't care about the multiverse. They care that Nuradyne turned their district into a "crime scene" and dumped their families in a relocation warehouse to rot. Ryker gives them a reason and a target; they give him the city's underside, the flooded sector, and bodies willing to fight Nuradyne security.

Neve and Toby anchor this thread as the human stakes: not abstractions across universes, but the specific people who live over the reactor. The relocation warehouse — overcrowded, underfunded, sealed indefinitely at Nuradyne's convenience — becomes the emblem of who pays. Any ending that keeps the domes lit keeps the siphon running; any ending that kills the siphon risks Neve and Toby's air. The player carries that the whole way.

## Mission 15 — Harmon, Cornered

Ryker goes back for Harmon — no longer to interrogate, now to *break.* Harmon is the man who saw abnormal readings before, assumed a drained universe was "trying something," took precautions he thought were enough, reported upward, and watched leadership suppress the reports. The previous incidents were containable. This one wasn't — a universe fought back and put a hole in his reactor and a saboteur in his city.

Harmon is not a monster. He's a competent man who chose comfort over conscience one memo at a time and is now terrified — of Nuradyne leadership, of the operative, of the "Authority" he's heard whispers of. Ryker can:
- **Turn him** — Harmon has the suppressed reports and the board-facing lie in full; handed to Tam or the CU board, they end Nuradyne's cover story. Turning Harmon requires convincing him he's already dead if he stays loyal (Voight cleans up witnesses, and Harmon is the biggest witness). His office computer holds the suppressed-report archive; reading it (player verb) triggers Tam's infolink with the board channel and the next objective.
- **Kill him** — simpler, satisfying, and it destroys the cleanest proof, forcing the harder endings.
- **Trade him to Voight** — the darkest option, buys Ryker room, and starts him down the road of becoming exactly the kind of asset the liaison was: knowledge for protection, one betrayal at a time.

## Mission 16 — Voight's Arithmetic

**Kira Voight** is the back half's best mirror-antagonist. She is not evil in her own frame. She believes, correctly, that Nuradyne's reactors are the only thing standing between Quinmore and a mass grave — lose the power, lose the domes, lose the air. She has known, or half-known, what the reactors really are, and decided the arithmetic favors the city she can see over the universes she can't. Everything she's done — the cordon, the cover-up, hunting the operative, cleaning up witnesses — proceeds from *if the reactors go down, everyone here dies.*

Ryker can fight her or flip her. Flipping her is the hardest, richest branch: he has to prove to her not that Nuradyne is dirty — she knows — but that the FMA has already **closed the book on Quinmore**, that her city is on a list to be abandoned and drained in its turn exactly like Sela's world, and that the arithmetic she's been running has a term she never had: *the predator she serves is also prey.* The proof is the FMA ledger Ryker pulled from the outpost — the sealed-substrates list with Quinmore on it. If she reads it (player verb, on a terminal Ryker delivers to her), it lands: protecting Nuradyne doesn't protect Quinmore; it just chooses the executioner. Voight flipped is the difference between several endings being survivable or not — she can hand Ryker Nuradyne's security infrastructure, or the reactor cores, or a seat at the CU board.

## Mission 17 — The Aperture Decision

Everything converges on the slum reactor's aperture — the door Sela came through, the wound in the world. Ryker has the siphon math (Marsh), the way to open an aperture (Sela), a corporate wedge to the board (Tam), the street (Ashline), the suppressed proof (Harmon), and maybe the Chief of Security (Voight). The FMA is watching from behind a sealing door for exactly one thing: whether its caretaker stays a caretaker or becomes a rogue node. Sable's last transmissions warn Ryker that forcing the aperture will bring a cleaner team — that the moment he tries to shout through the door, the door shouts back. Ryker does it anyway, or doesn't. The endings are the shape of that choice.

# The Central Twists (called out)

**Twist 1 — The Caretaker Clause (new to this project).** Ryker was never sent to investigate and come home. The FMA's doctrine for a universe that has developed or acquired dangerous multiversal capability is not to police it but to **seal it and abandon it**, staffing it with a single deliberately-stranded resident agent — chosen for having no ties, no way home, and no option but to manage the box from inside. The canon liaison's "stranded by the war" is the *cover story* of a decommissioned caretaker; Ryker is his active replacement, posting **CTKR-7134**, repatriation N/A, dated before the prologue. The FMA doesn't stop the horror. It walks away from it and leaves a man to watch. Delivered entirely through conversations (Coordinator, Marsh) and one readable mission file — no forbidden verbs.

**Twist 2 — Sealing manufactures the monster (new siphon-horror layer).** The drained universe that traced the siphon and sent the operative — the canon inciting incident — is itself a universe the FMA **sealed and abandoned decades ago.** Cut off, forgotten, then drained to death from a world it couldn't see, it did the only thing an abandoned world can: it clawed back. The FMA's containment doctrine is self-defeating — every sealed universe becomes a desperate, retaliating rogue node, and Quinmore is next on the list to become one. The abandonment is not the story's background; it's the *cause* of the reactor explosion Ryker was sent to investigate. Delivered through Sela's conversation and the outpost's sealed-substrates ledger.

Both twists interlock: the doctrine that strands Ryker is the same doctrine that created Sela's world, and the same doctrine now condemning Quinmore. The mirror runs from one man up to a whole universe.

# Factions & Power Structure

- **The FMA.** Not a cartel and not a conspiracy — a bureaucracy old enough to have replaced judgment with policy. It genuinely believes sealing is prudent containment; it cannot see that abandonment breeds the exact threats it fears, because the wreckage always falls on the far side of a closed door. Cares about: network stability, not being on the hook. Fatal flaw: it treats universes and agents as sunk costs. Represented by the **Coordinator** (the policy), **Sable** (the conscience it strands), and, offstage, the cleaner team it will send if Ryker becomes a node.
- **Nuradyne.** Runs the power grid, therefore the city. Inner circle knows the reactors siphon; the CU board doesn't. Cares about: keeping the secret and the grid. **Harmon** (guilt), **Voight** (arithmetic), leadership (suppression). Leverage over every other corp because everyone needs power.
- **Atlas Subsystems.** Builds the shielding, resents its dependence on Nuradyne, suspects a power play. Cares about: leverage and survival, in that order. **Rhona Tam** (a real conscience inside a corporate agenda). Ryker's wedge to the board.
- **The CU board.** The only authority above Nuradyne. Approved the reactors as ordinary tech. Slow, self-interested, but capable of ending Nuradyne if shown fraud against the Union — and capable of simply installing itself as the new owner of the siphon.
- **The slums / Ashline.** The written-off. No stake in the multiverse, total stake in the air over their heads. **Bex**, the crew, **Neve** and **Toby.** The human ledger every ending is measured against.
- **The drained universes.** Silent victims made monstrous by abandonment. **Sela** is the one that fought back — the FMA's past sin, walking around Ryker's present.
- **Marsh, the Ferryman.** A faction of one: the FMA's discarded caretaker, the story's living warning, and the only man who understands both the siphon and the clause.

# Key Characters

- **Liam Ryker.** Blank-slate, low-affect, morally flexible ex-military. Chosen *because* he's empty — no ties to pull him home, no self to object to being spent. The story weaponizes the player-avatar trait: the thing that makes Ryker a good vessel for the player is the exact thing that made him a good man to abandon. His arc is whether a man built to be disposable can choose to matter.
- **The Coordinator.** Unhurried, decent-seeming, the human face of policy. Never lies to Ryker — just never tells him the mission's real tense.
- **Sable.** The partner. Follows orders, then has to live behind a closing door with what the orders did. The conscience-in-a-box; a preview of what caretaking does to a person.
- **Elias Marsh (the Ferryman).** The canon liaison. The mirror. Sold the sky to survive, and has spent years unable to die of it. Ryker's future if Ryker stays a caretaker; Ryker's teacher if Ryker won't.
- **Sela.** The canon operative. Not a terrorist — a woman from an abandoned world, wrapped in a field that keeps her alive and cages her, come to kill the thing draining her people. The FMA's past, retaliating. Ryker's mirror across the aperture: two people the FMA stranded, on opposite sides of a door.
- **Director Harmon.** Competence corrupted by comfort. The suppressed reports live on his computer and in his conscience.
- **Kira Voight.** The best antagonist because she's right about the thing she can see and blind to the thing she can't. Flippable, and worth flipping.
- **Rhona Tam.** Atlas engineer, real conscience, corporate agenda. Ally as far as interests align.
- **Bex, Neve, Toby.** The street, the mother, the boy. The reason any of it matters at ground level.

# Player-Choice Structure & Endings

Choices compound: who lives (Marsh, Harmon, Sela, Voight), who Ryker allied with (Atlas / Ashline / a flipped Voight / no one), and whether he ever accepts the caretaker posting or fights it. Four ideological endings, each a different answer to the mirror.

**Ending A — The Caretaker (Acceptance).** Ryker stops fighting the clause and takes the posting — but on his own terms. He becomes Quinmore's hidden resident officer, using Marsh's knowledge and the siphon math to throttle the reactors down slowly, keep Nuradyne honest from the shadows, and manage the box the FMA left him. The domes stay lit. The drained universes keep dying, slower. Ryker becomes the Ferryman, completed — a better version, maybe, but the same shape. The bleakest and quietest ending: the system wins by being survivable. Marsh's line over the last shot: *"That's the trap, son. It's not that they make you do it. It's that doing it is the only thing that keeps you alive, and eventually you call that a choice."*

**Ending B — Break the Seal (Idealist Gamble).** Ryker uses Sela's aperture tech to force the sealed door open and broadcast everything — the siphon, the clause, the ledger — into the FMA network, betting that not all of the FMA endorses abandonment, that there are factions who'd be sickened to learn what their doctrine breeds. It might ignite reform. It might just bring the cleaner team. The ending leaves it genuinely uncertain — the shout goes out, the door answers, and the last image is Ryker standing in the aperture-light not knowing if he lit a fire or a fuse. The only ending that treats the FMA as something that can be *changed* rather than escaped or served.

**Ending C — Burn the Siphon (Ends-Justify-the-Means, at scale).** Ryker allies fully with Sela and the Ashline and destroys the reactors — every straw pulled out of every world at once. The drained universes are freed; Sela's people get their generation. Quinmore's domes go dark. If Ryker flipped Voight and moved early, it's a desperate mass exodus and a survivable catastrophe; if he didn't, it's Neve and Toby's air, and he did it anyway because the arithmetic across universes is not close. The purest expression of Ryker's "just business" flexibility taken to its terminal conclusion: he weighs the many he can't see against the few he can, and chooses the many, and has to live under the same poisoned sky he left everyone else. The most morally violent ending.

**Ending D — The New Liaison (Cynical Mirror).** Ryker hands the reactor truth — and himself — to Atlas and the CU board, toppling Nuradyne. But the board doesn't shut down a working siphon; it takes it over. Atlas, or the Union itself, becomes the new owner of the straw in the world's side, cleaner-handed, better-managed, exactly as lethal to the universes downstream. Ryker gets what Marsh once got: protection, a life inside the domes, a seat near power in exchange for what he knows. He becomes the liaison — not the FMA's this time, the Union's — and the siphon runs on under new management. The ending that completes the original sin instead of the doctrine: the man sent to expose the theft becomes its next quiet keeper. The final infolink is silence — the FMA doesn't even bother to send the cleaner team, because a caretaker who's gone corporate is still, functionally, doing the job. The book stays closed. It was always going to.

Across all four, the same mirror: Marsh sold the sky and was left; Sela was left and clawed back; Ryker is offered the chance to be left, to leave, to burn it down, or to sell the sky himself. The FMA's one doctrine produced every person in the room. The player decides whether Ryker breaks the pattern or takes his place in it.

# Themes

- **Abandonment as policy.** The horror isn't a villain's cruelty; it's an institution's arithmetic. The FMA doesn't hate the worlds it seals. It just stops answering, and calls the silence containment. The cruelest thing in the story is a closed door with good paperwork.
- **The disposable man.** Ryker is a blank slate so the player can inhabit him — and the story makes that emptiness the reason he was chosen to be spent. Being no one is what qualifies you to be sacrificed. The arc is a man built to be used deciding what, if anything, he's for.
- **Mirrors all the way up.** The liaison, the operative, the partner, the caretaker, and eventually a whole universe are the same wound at different scales. Quinmore is scheduled to become Sela's world; Ryker is scheduled to become Marsh. The plot is the refusal — or acceptance — of the reflection.
- **The unseen ledger.** Every faction runs an arithmetic that leaves out the term it can't see: Nuradyne leaves out the drained universes, Voight leaves out that the predator is prey, the FMA leaves out that abandonment breeds monsters. Only the player, standing where all the terminals meet, ever sees the whole equation — and even then, has to choose which lives to leave out of it.
