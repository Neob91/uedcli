# AFTERIMAGE

Writer: sonnet | Angle: street-level insurgency + memory/identity mystery

## Logline

In a city where the streetlights read your mind as much as they light your way, a resistance courier who can't remember her own life before three years ago is sent to steal proof of the program that edits memories — and starts to suspect the proof is her.

## Premise

New York has been rebuilt twice in twenty years — first by money, then by light. After the Brownout, a private-public authority called the Ward strung the boroughs with an adaptive streetlamp grid, LUM, sold to a frightened city as crime-suppression: lights that brighten around trouble, dim for curfew, paint the safe paths home. What the public wasn't told is that LUM's flicker isn't just illumination. Coded into its pulse is a slow neural entrainment signal — mood management at first, then compliance, then, for a handful of deniable assets, targeted memory revision. Vee, a courier for the resistance network called the Underglow, has run drops through the dead zones between lamps for three years. She remembers joining. She remembers her first cell, her first arrest, her first kill. None of it happened to the person she actually is. It happened to a story Vantage Dynamics wrote and switched on inside her skull, over whoever she used to be — and whoever that was, the program made sure there'd be nothing left to miss her.

## Setting & world

Fifteen years post-Brownout: a three-day citywide blackout during a heat riot that killed 900 people in stalled subway cars and unlit stairwells. The trauma of that week is the load-bearing myth the whole city runs on. The Ward — officially the Ward Authority for Public Illumination and Order — rose out of the emergency committees that restored power, and never fully handed the city back. LUM, their grid, is genuinely loved by half the population: it works, crime dropped, the trains run lit again. The other half lives in the Narrows — the old waterfront, the flooded subway levels, the blocks LUM's coverage maps mark "low-priority" and leave dim on purpose, because dim is where the Ward can't see and can't be blamed for what happens there. The Narrows is where the Underglow lives: a loose network of people who've learned to read the grid's blind intervals and move through them, running food, medicine, and dissent past a lighting system that is also, unmistakably, a nervous system with the city wired into it.

The tech: LUM lamps pulse in patterns invisible to the naked eye but readable by a common cheap ocular implant called a flicker-lens — originally sold as an epilepsy-safety aug, repurposed on the street into a way to see the grid's intent before it sees you. The same interface, at higher gain and closer range, is Vantage Dynamics' real product: Palimpsest, a light-based protocol for writing and overwriting memory. Officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially, it is how the Ward manufactures its most reliable informants — not people who were bribed or broken, but people who were built.

## Factions

**The Ward Authority.** Public security force, genuinely popular uptown, genuinely feared in the Narrows. Runs LUM, runs the curfews, runs a Behavioral Systems division that officially studies "civic phototherapy" and unofficially runs Palimpsest. Believes, at every level except the very top, that it is holding the city together. Agenda: keep the lights on, keep the Narrows contained, keep the myth of the Brownout doing its work.

**The Underglow.** Not an army — a network of cells, couriers, safehouses, and one shared conviction that the dark isn't a threat to be lit but a right to be defended. Split into two wings that hate each other more than they hate the Ward. The moderate wing, run by Tomas Reyes, wants to break LUM's mind-control layer while keeping the grid's actual lights on, because half the Narrows still needs streetlight to survive the night. The hard wing, the Cinders, led by Dalia "Ozone" Vance, wants every lamp in the city dead, full stop, on the theory that a grid this compromised can't be reformed, only killed.

**Vantage Dynamics.** The contractor that built LUM and owns Palimpsest outright. Publicly a lighting-and-infrastructure firm. Its Behavioral Systems liaison to the Ward, Director Aldous Kesh, is the only person alive who can see the whole board — because he built both halves of it.

**The Reform Ward** (emergent, mid-story). A handful of Ward officers, led by patrol commander Dara Okonkwo, who joined to keep people safe and are starting to notice the gap between that and what Behavioral Systems actually does.

## Protagonist

Vee — no surname she trusts — runs courier jobs for the Underglow's Narrows cell: contraband insulin, forged transit passes, warnings passed hand to hand because phones are read by the grid. She's good at her job specifically because of a flicker-lens implant sharper than the street-market kind, which she's always assumed was a lucky salvage. She has three years of vivid, textured memory: the safehouse where Reyes found her half-starved and taught her to read lamp intervals, the first patrol she outran, the friend she lost to a Ward sweep. She also has holes — missing hours here and there, always around bright light, always written off as an old head injury nobody's bothered to properly explain to her. At the start she believes what everyone around her believes: that she's a survivor who chose a side. By the end she has to decide whether choosing a side is still possible for someone who's been written twice, and whether that even matters — whether the choosing is the self, not the origin.

## Full plot

### Act One — The Narrows

Vee runs a routine drop: insulin to a tenement past Curfew Line Seven, timed to a gap in the LUM pulse cycle her flicker-lens reads two seconds ahead of anyone else's. On the way back she takes a shortcut under a dead transformer and gets a flicker — not from the lens, from inside her: a half-second flash of a chair, restraints, a bank of white light too even to be a lamp, and a voice reciting numbers. It's gone before she can hold it. She logs it as a migraine and tells no one, because couriers who report head trouble get pulled off routes.

Reyes gives her the real job: Elias Farrow, a Ward lighting engineer with Behavioral Systems clearance, wants to defect and bring proof that LUM does more than manage traffic and mood. Vee and hacker Priya "Prish" Nandakumar are sent to pull him out of a substation on the edge of Ward territory before his access gets revoked. The extraction goes sideways — Farrow's already burned, Ward response is faster than it should be for a routine defection — and in the three minutes before he's shot through a chain-link fence, Farrow looks straight at Vee and says a name that isn't hers. "Subject Twelve. Kessler. You're not — " He doesn't finish it. Vee gets the drive he was carrying out anyway and gets Prish out alive.

Back at the safehouse, Prish can't crack the drive's outer shell but pulls enough metadata to confirm it's Palimpsest documentation — protocol notes, subject logs, a phrase repeated across files: identity overlay, non-consensual, field-durable. Reyes wants to sit on it and plan. Ozone's people want to hit a relay tower immediately, argue the drive is proof enough that the whole grid needs to die before it writes over one more person. Vee says nothing about the name Farrow called her. She tells herself it isn't relevant.

*Player choice, first branch:* back Reyes's caution and spend the interlude building trust with the moderate cell, or run with the Cinders on a smaller sabotage job to prove the network's teeth — this shapes who trusts Vee later and unlocks different approach options at the climax.

### Act Two — Reading the Light

Ozone's faction wants the Farrow drive cracked fast, and the only way to do that is to get closer to a live Palimpsest terminal — which means casing a Ward substation from the inside. Vee, whose flicker-lens reads Ward patrol patterns better than anyone in the cell, is sent in disguised as a phototherapy patient, a legitimate cover the Ward runs for citizens with grid-sensitivity complaints. Inside, patrol commander Dara Okonkwo does a routine ID check and pauses half a second too long on Vee's face — not suspicion exactly, something closer to recognition she can't place. Vee gets what she needs (relay schematics, terminal access windows) and gets out, but the pause stays with her.

The cell debates the tower strike. Reyes wants a surgical hit: kill the Palimpsest relay feeding one district, leave the streetlights themselves running, spare the people in the Narrows who need them to get home safe at night. Ozone wants the whole tower down — argues that a "surgical" hit just teaches the Ward to harden the next one, and that half-measures are how the Underglow has lost every fight that mattered.

*Player choice, second branch:* support the surgical strike, back the full demolition, or — if the player has been quietly Ward-curious — leak the plan to Okonkwo's people in exchange for information about Palimpsest, a betrayal Reyes will eventually find out about one way or another.

Whichever the player picks, the operation goes wrong at the terminal room itself: the moment Vee jacks a cable into the relay to pull data, the room's lights snap to a specific stutter pattern she's never consciously seen before — and her body answers it. She freezes, then moves through a sequence of tool use and cable routing she has no memory of learning, faster and cleaner than her training accounts for. Prish, watching over comms, clocks it and says nothing at the time. Ward security catches the team mid-extraction. Reyes gets everyone but Vee out. She's taken.

### Act Three — Subject Twelve

Vee wakes in a Behavioral Systems chair — the same chair from her earlier flicker, restraints and all, the room's light banked to the exact white she half-remembered. Director Aldous Kesh interviews her personally, calm, almost gentle, and lets slip enough — a date, a designation, a photograph of a woman who is unmistakably her wearing a Ward analyst's badge — that the picture assembles itself whether she wants it to or not.

**Twist one.** Vee is Mara Kessler, a Ward Behavioral Systems analyst who three years ago either volunteered for or was conscripted into an experimental deep-cover Palimpsest deployment: full identity overlay, planted as a defector, built to rise inside the Underglow and report back through dead-drop intervals she doesn't consciously access. Every memory she has of the last three years — the safehouse, Reyes finding her, the friend she lost — is a written narrative layered over Mara Kessler the way paint covers a wall, and Mara Kessler herself was never told the operation would erase her this completely; the "controlled overlay" was supposed to leave a supervised through-line back to her real self. It didn't. The drive Farrow died for is the record of that failure, and of eleven other subjects who came before her, none of whom got their real names back.

Kesh doesn't want to punish her. He wants to fix the overlay and redeploy her — reset "Vee," patch the gap Farrow's leak tore open, send her back into the Narrows cleaner and more durable than before. He starts the procedure. It's interrupted — a Reform Ward strike team under Okonkwo, who has been quietly building her own case against Behavioral Systems since the substation ID check made her realize she recognized "the patient's" gait from a training-exercise personnel file she wasn't supposed to remember either — hits the facility looking for evidence of a different abuse entirely and, in the chaos, Vee gets out half-reset: neither Vee nor Kessler cleanly, both memory sets active and fighting each other, nothing about her past reliable anymore except the fact that none of it is original.

### Act Four — The Architect

Vee returns to the Narrows a stranger to herself. Prish, who clocked the terminal-room glitch, isn't surprised by what Vee tells her, and starts digging through the Farrow drive with new urgency. Reyes's reaction is the second gut-punch of the story: he already suspected. Not the details, not the name Kessler, but he'd been warned three years ago by his own mentor — a man he's simply called "the Architect" since before Vee ever showed up — that a woman fitting her description might come through the network carrying "old ink," and that he should watch her, not burn her, because "how a person chooses to be, once they know, matters more than what they were built as." Reyes kept her anyway, on faith in that principle. He never told her. That omission — not malice, but a bet made with someone else's life — is its own betrayal, and the player can choose how much Vee forgives it.

Prish finally breaks the drive's core layer, and the second twist lands. The Architect Reyes has trusted for a decade, the man who half-raised the Underglow's founding cells and taught them to read the dark, is Aldous Kesh. Kesh didn't just build Palimpsest — thirty years ago, as a young Ward planner, he seeded the Underglow's earliest safehouse network himself, under a different name, because a security state needs a resistance to justify its budget and its grid, and a resistance that's secretly shepherded is safer, and more useful for testing overlay subjects on, than one that grows wild. Reyes was recruited into leadership the way Vee was recruited into cover: chosen young, cultivated, never told the hand on his shoulder was the same hand signing Behavioral Systems' budget uptown. The Underglow and the Ward were never really at war. They were two departments of the same experiment, and Kesh has spent thirty years making sure neither side found out — right up until Farrow did, and paid for it, and Vee found out by accident that she was the proof.

### Act Five — The Anchor

The Farrow drive, fully cracked, gives Prish the location of Vantage's central node — the Anchor, where LUM's citywide pulse originates and where the Palimpsest archive lives, including, Vee is fairly sure, whatever's left of Mara Kessler's actual pre-overlay memories, if any survived being written over eleven times before her. Kesh, expecting this, is waiting there rather than fleeing — he wants to make Vee an offer in person, and the story's climax is built around what she does with it, not around a firefight she can walk into with Reyes, Prish, Okonkwo's Reform Ward cell, Ozone's Cinders, or alone, depending on who she's kept faith with through the branches above.

Kesh's offer: a full clean reset, no Vee, no Kessler, a new name and a real chance at an unwritten life, in exchange for the drive and her silence. He's not lying about being able to do it. He's also not lying that it would, for the first time, actually be her choice.

## Key characters

**Vee / Mara Kessler** — protagonist. Courier, then discovered asset, then a woman with two competing pasts and no way to verify either. Her flicker-lens implant, the thing that makes her exceptional at her job, turns out to be the exact hardware Palimpsest uses to write her — the gift and the leash are the same object.

**Tomas Reyes** — Underglow cell leader, found Vee (as far as she remembers) starving in a flooded platform and trained her. Genuinely loves the people in his cell. Has been managed his whole adult life by Kesh without knowing it, and made one deliberate, unconfessed bet on Vee's behalf that he never gave her the chance to accept or refuse. Wants reform: kill Palimpsest, keep the streetlights.

**Priya "Prish" Nandakumar** — hacker, the network's actual conscience. Trusts evidence over loyalty, which is why she's the one who breaks the drive open and the one Vee tells the truth to first. Doesn't forgive easily but doesn't lie either.

**Dalia "Ozone" Vance** — Cinders leader. Lost family in the Brownout's dark stairwells and again, later, to a LUM-flagged Ward sweep; believes any grid this compromised is a knife with no safe handle. Not a villain — the story's clearest ideological purist, and the person most likely to be right that half-measures don't survive contact with power.

**Lieutenant Dara Okonkwo** — Ward patrol commander, believer in the mission who starts noticing the gaps between what she signed up for and what Behavioral Systems does under the same badge. Her personnel-file recognition of Vee is the first crack; by Act Four she's running her own quiet internal investigation and can become an ally.

**Elias Farrow** — Ward lighting engineer, the story's inciting whistleblower. Dies in Act One having said just enough to break Vee's certainty about herself, never enough to explain it — his death is the mystery's engine, not its answer.

**Director Aldous Kesh** — the Architect. Runs Vantage Behavioral Systems and, under decades of cover, founded the Underglow's earliest cells. Not cartoonishly evil — genuinely believes a managed conflict between a security grid and a shepherded resistance keeps the city more stable than either an unchecked Ward or a wild insurgency would. Willing to erase individual people to keep that balance running, and sees Vee's case as an engineering setback, not a moral one.

## The central twists

**Twist one — you are the file you're looking for.** The player spends Act One and Two investigating a memory-editing conspiracy as an outsider; Act Three reveals the protagonist is herself an overlaid subject, and the "three years of memory" the player has been playing through as backstory-in-progress was never original to begin with. This lands hardest if the player has leaned into Vee's Underglow loyalty in the early branches — the game was quietly asking you to invest in an identity it always intended to dissolve.

**Twist two — the war was administered, not fought.** The Underglow's founding wasn't organic resistance; it was seeded and has been quietly shepherded for thirty years by the same man running the Ward's mind-editing program, so that a security state and its opposition could keep justifying each other's budgets and recruiting pools. Reyes, the player's most trusted ally, was cultivated the same way Vee was written — he's just old enough to have made his own choices inside the frame Kesh built for him, which is precisely why his one unconfessed bet on Vee's behalf reads as a betrayal even though his instincts were, in the end, decent ones.

**The identity twist underneath both.** Palimpsest doesn't just overlay one false memory on one true self — Mara Kessler's own pre-overlay memories, when Prish finally gets a partial trace on the archive, turn out to be fragmentary and partially synthetic themselves, an earlier subject's cover life reused. There is no clean original to restore. The story's real question isn't "who was I really," which has no answer, but "who do I choose to be now that I know there's no bedrock" — and that's the question every ending forces the player to actually decide, not the trivia of Vee's birth name.

## Player-choice structure & endings

Choices accumulate across three tracked axes rather than a single morality meter: **Trust** (Reyes/Underglow moderates vs. Ozone/Cinders vs. Okonkwo/Reform Ward), **Exposure** (how much of the Palimpsest truth Vee shares with each ally as she learns it, versus what she keeps to control the narrative herself), and **Self** (whether Vee tries to recover/verify her original identity, accepts the Vee identity as functionally real regardless of origin, or actively refuses to resolve the question). These determine which allies are alive and present at the Anchor, how much leverage Vee has over Kesh, and which of four endings is reachable.

**Ending A — Burn the Grid** *(high Trust-Cinders, high Exposure)*: Vee sides with Ozone, destroys the Anchor outright. LUM goes dark citywide, not just the Narrows. The Ward's control collapses with it; so does every streetlight the Narrows actually depended on to get people home safe. Freedom bought at the cost of the very infrastructure the Underglow's less ideological members needed — a city plunged back toward Brownout conditions, with the Underglow now the only organized power left standing, and every reason to become what it fought.

**Ending B — Seize the Light** *(high Trust-Reyes, high Exposure)*: Vee and Prish go public with the Farrow drive and Kesh's dual identity, force a Ward-Underglow reckoning brokered by Okonkwo's Reform faction. LUM stays lit but loses its covert conditioning layer; oversight passes to an uneasy coalition of reformist Ward officers and Underglow moderates. Slow, compromised, plausible — the story's closest thing to a stable good ending, and explicitly imperfect: Ozone's people call it collaboration and split off, and nobody promises the coalition holds.

**Ending C — Wear the Mask** *(low Exposure, high Trust-Ward or player accepts Kesh's frame)*: Vee takes Kesh's offer, or a version of it — either lets herself be reset clean and disappears into the machine as a new deniable asset, or, in the story's darkest branch, takes Kesh's place herself, convinced that someone competent and merciful has to run the balance or someone worse will. Order is preserved. So is the machinery that produced her. The ending plays the same institutional continuity as a horror, not a triumph, whichever seat she takes.

**Ending D — The Unwritten Self** *(high Self-refusal, moderate/low Exposure)*: Vee refuses every faction's claim on her — doesn't burn the grid, doesn't reform it, doesn't hand herself back to Kesh — and instead destroys only her own Palimpsest file, denying everyone, including the player, a clean answer about who she "really" was. She walks out of the Anchor into the Narrows' dim edge and disappears from every faction's map. The city's fate is left open, mid-conflict, unresolved by design. The last scene is Vee, unlabeled, choosing what to do with an ordinary hour for what may be the first time — the story's argument that identity was never a file to recover, only a practice to keep doing.

## Themes

Surveillance sold as care, and how convincingly "the light is for your safety" can be true and also be a leash. The manufactured nature of a lot of "organic" conflict — how institutions can profit from opposition enough to quietly cultivate it, on both sides of a line they've drawn. And under both: memory and identity as authored rather than excavated — the story deliberately withholds a "real" Mara Kessler to return to, because its actual claim is that selfhood was never a fact buried under the overlays, it's the ongoing, revisable act of choosing what to do next, done by whoever's doing the choosing, origin be damned.
